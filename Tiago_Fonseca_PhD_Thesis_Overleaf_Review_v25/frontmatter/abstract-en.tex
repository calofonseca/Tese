\frontmatterchapter{Abstract}

Real-world cyber-physical systems couple software decisions to physical processes. Industrial plants, autonomous robots, transport networks, smart buildings, and energy communities all depend on controllers whose decisions affect equipment, users, and shared infrastructure. These controllers are usually developed and tested in simulation, where participants, interfaces, data quality, constraints, and computing resources can be kept stable. Those assumptions weaken when a controller reaches a real installation. Assets appear or become unavailable, observations arrive late or incomplete, feasible actions change, and an error can affect service or equipment. Good simulated performance therefore provides only part of the evidence needed to establish real-world applicability.

This thesis studies that gap using Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) as the control approach for distributed decision-making. MARL is relevant when local agents act with limited information but their decisions interact through shared resources and collective objectives. Renewable Energy Communities and electric-vehicle charging provide the empirical setting because they combine heterogeneous participants, flexible assets, user deadlines, individual economic interests, and common electrical limits. The investigation is structured by four connected requirements, namely scalability, deployability, safety, and robustness.

EnergAIze connects system and algorithm design across problem representation, data, simulation, controller development, experimentation, and deployment. CityLearn v3 and annual 15-minute datasets make heterogeneity, constraints, faults, and reconfiguration reproducible. A shared protocol compares rule-based, optimisation, single-agent, and multi-agent methods and exposes their assumptions. TI-MARL (Typed-Interface Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning) is the principal algorithmic contribution. It represents each agent's current interface as a versioned typed contract bound to identifiable entities. A deterministic compiler derives valid observations, action groups, dependencies, and bounds. A shared decentralised policy operates over this variable interface, a variable-population critic supports coordination during training, and an analytic projector enforces local feasibility.

The evaluation covers reconfiguration, faults, scalability, compatibility, heterogeneous runtimes, OPEVA, and DemFlex. The evidence indicates that real-world applicability depends on preserving the meaning of observations, actions, constraints, and policy artefacts across simulation and execution. TI-MARL supports reconfiguration among known semantic types, while lifecycle and assurance mechanisms preserve traceability, feasibility, and recovery. The conclusions remain bounded to the evaluated energy systems and do not claim unknown-type admission, universal formal safety, or autonomous deployment of coordinated policy sets.
