In many real-world systems, participants make decisions about their own resources while sharing the consequences with others, so a choice that serves one participant well may limit what another can achieve. A shared goal therefore has to accommodate local interests that sometimes pull in different directions. The same difficulty appears in robot teams, networks of road junctions, and groups of buildings managing electricity together, where local decisions contribute to an outcome that no participant determines alone. \gls{marl} offers a way to learn these relationships through experience, allowing agents to improve their decisions in response to both their own actions and those of others \parencite{gronauer2022survey,keren2024energynetworks}.

This thesis investigates how such learned coordination can be put to use in real-world systems by developing multi-agent learning and control methods alongside the mechanisms needed to apply their decisions in practice. The central problem is to translate \gls{marl}'s promising results in simulation into controllers that organisations can deploy and maintain, with \glspl{rec} providing the setting for this investigation. The following sections establish the context and motivation for the work, formulate the research problem, and set out the thesis statement and \glspl{rq}. The contributions and organisation of the thesis complete this introductory chapter.

\section{Context and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-context}

To place this investigation in context, the discussion begins with \gls{marl} and the ways in which agents learn to coordinate their decisions. It then turns to energy management, starting with the needs and flexibility of a single household before considering how its choices interact with those of other members of a \gls{rec}. These interactions bring shared objectives into contact with equipment limits and individual service requirements, revealing the coordination challenges that make this setting well suited to investigating \gls{marl} under real-world conditions.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Learning and Control}
\label{sec:intro-marl}

Control involves choosing actions that guide a system towards an intended outcome, using the available information about its current condition and how it may evolve. When several controllers act within the same environment, each decision can influence both the part of the system being controlled and the conditions faced by others. Coordination requires these effects to be considered together \parencite{albrecht2024marl}.

Neighbouring road junctions, for example, illustrate how an action can affect conditions elsewhere in the system. Keeping a traffic light green for longer may clear the queue at one junction while sending more vehicles towards the next, so the value of the decision depends on both the traffic that can move immediately and the conditions created further along the road.

The way a controller responds to these conditions can be specified through different approaches. It may operate with rules defined by an expert, optimise an explicit model, or search among candidate schedules. This thesis focuses on learning because experience can help improve decisions whose consequences emerge over time and depend on other participants \parencite{sutton2018rl,albrecht2024marl}. Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work} compares learning with other approaches to these control problems, including rule-based and optimisation-based methods.

\gls{rl} formalises this use of experience through the interaction between a decision-maker, termed an \emph{agent}, and its environment. The agent receives \emph{observations} and selects an \emph{action} according to a \emph{policy}, while a numerical \emph{reward} expresses how the outcome relates to the intended objective. By seeking to improve the reward accumulated over time, learning gives consequences that emerge later a role in the present decision \parencite{sutton2018rl}. At a road junction, observations might describe the number of waiting vehicles and the current signal phases, while actions adjust the timing of the lights. Through repeated interactions, the controller learns how these adjustments affect subsequent traffic.

With several learning controllers, the experience of one agent also reflects the decisions of others, whose behaviour may change as they learn. Traffic arriving at a junction, for example, depends on what neighbouring controllers have released. With the objective of reducing delay across the road network, \gls{marl} seeks to learn how local decisions can contribute to this shared outcome. Each controller must account for these wider consequences while observing and acting on its own part of the system \parencite{gronauer2022survey,albrecht2024marl}.

The prospect of learning coordinated behaviour has encouraged research on \gls{marl} in cooperative robot navigation, task allocation, traffic control, and complex strategy games, with surveys reporting promising results across these domains \parencite{gronauer2022survey,orr2023multirobot}. Once trained, a policy can draw on experience gathered through many interactions to select an action from current observations. Translating experimental success into reliable real-world operation nevertheless remains a research challenge, since the learned behaviour must function with the information, constraints, and equipment of an actual installation \parencite{dulacarnold2021realworld,orr2023multirobot}. This is the central problem pursued in this thesis and developed in Section~\ref{sec:intro-problem}.

Energy management provides a setting in which both the promise of coordination and the demands of practical control can be examined. Decisions have consequences over time and across participants, as electricity stored in one building can meet a later need, while electricity consumed there is no longer available for another use \parencite{keren2024energynetworks}. Understanding these relationships begins with the choices available within a single household.

\subsection{Energy Management and Flexibility in Homes}
\label{sec:intro-energy-flexibility}

For a household, energy management is closely tied to the rhythm of daily life. Rooms should be warm when occupants return, a dishwasher may have until morning to finish its cycle, and a vehicle needs enough energy for the next journey. These requirements leave different amounts of freedom over when equipment operates. Solar panels and batteries extend that freedom by allowing some electricity to be produced at home and retained for later use. The controller's task is to arrange these choices around the services the occupants expect.

Consider an \gls{ev} that returns home at 18:00 with its battery at a \gls{soc} of 30\%. The driver plans to leave at 08:00 the following morning and requests a departure \gls{soc} of 80\%. This specifies the service to be delivered, but leaves fourteen hours in which to arrange charging. Charging immediately would draw power alongside cooking, heating, and other evening uses, adding to demand at a time when the household connection may already be heavily used. If many residents charge in this way, the concentration can also increase pressure on the local network and the need for costly infrastructure reinforcement \parencite{hodge2017charging}.

Those fourteen hours allow charging to be arranged around the household's other needs. \gls{ev} charging power can be reduced during the evening peak and increased after demand has fallen, or part of the required energy can be delivered during lower-price hours. This freedom to adjust consumption, storage, or supply within operating limits while preserving the required service is termed \emph{energy flexibility} \parencite{hussain2023flexibility}. One representation, the \emph{FlexOffer}, describes flexible energy amounts and timing and is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:ch2-rec-flexibility} \parencite{siksnys2019flexoffers}.

For the vehicle, the available flexibility depends on how much energy is still needed, how long it will remain connected, the vehicle and charger ratings, and the capacity available at the household connection. As departure approaches, there is less room to postpone charging. Other appliances offer different choices, with a dishwasher allowing its start time to move while a heat pump must keep the indoor temperature within an acceptable range. Each resource therefore combines an opportunity to adjust energy use with a requirement that the adjustment must preserve.

Local energy production changes which of those choices are attractive. Rooftop \gls{pv} panels may produce a surplus around midday, several hours before the household's evening demand. For the vehicle arriving at 18:00, a stationary battery offers a way to retain some of that production for later use. On a different day, when the same vehicle remains at home until late afternoon, charging can coincide directly with solar generation while still meeting its next departure target. A flexible appliance provides another use for the surplus. The timing of demand and storage therefore helps determine how much renewable electricity is used in the home and how much electricity must be bought or exported \parencite{hussain2023flexibility,hodge2017charging}.

The relationships introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro-marl} are visible in these household choices, where the vehicle, storage, and flexible appliances share the available energy and connection capacity over time. A household may prioritise lower expenditure, greater use of locally produced electricity, reduced demand peaks, or a chosen combination of these aims. Whichever objective is selected, comfort and the energy needed for the next journey remain service requirements. The preferred timing then depends on what the present decision makes possible later. Filling the stationary battery with cheaper grid electricity overnight may reduce subsequent purchases but leave less room for the next day's solar surplus; using its stored energy early in the evening leaves less for a later demand peak. A household controller must relate these consequences to the chosen objective, using measurements and forecasts of demand, generation, and prices \parencite{chen2022rlpower}.

For a single household, these decisions can be learned through \gls{rl} by one agent managing its controllable resources \parencite{vazquezcanteli2019demandresponse}. In practice, meters, sensors, and device interfaces connect that agent to the home's equipment, providing measurements and carrying its commands. Section~\ref{sec:intro-problem} discusses the practical challenges of this sensing and control architecture.

Some opportunities extend beyond the household. While its occupants are away and its panels produce a surplus, a neighbour may have an \gls{ev} ready to charge. Using that surplus introduces a coordination problem between households, extending the decisions previously considered within a single home.

\subsection{Renewable Energy Communities}
\label{sec:intro-rec}

For neighbouring households to make use of such opportunities, they need an arrangement through which they can participate, share energy, and agree on the benefits they seek. \glspl{rec} provide this setting. They are autonomous legal entities with open and voluntary participation, bringing together individuals, small and medium-sized enterprises, and local authorities around renewable generation, consumption, storage, and sharing. Their primary purpose is to provide environmental, economic, or social benefits to their members and the local area \parencite{eu2018redii,portugal2022electricity}. Differences in members' routines and equipment then become a resource for coordination, allowing production at one building to meet demand at another \parencite{reis2021businessmodels}.

The composition of a \gls{rec} can change both through members joining or leaving and through changes to the equipment within each household. Existing members may install new chargers, batteries, or other assets, remove equipment, or face temporary unavailability when a device fails. Vehicles connecting and disconnecting also change which resources can participate at a given time. These changes in resources, capabilities, and relationships constitute \emph{dynamic reconfiguration}, altering the opportunities for sharing and the actions available for coordination.

Returning to the example of the vehicle that remains at home until late afternoon, its charging can draw on another member's solar surplus where sharing arrangements permit. \glspl{bess} at participating buildings can retain production for later needs, extending the opportunities to use renewable electricity within the \gls{rec} \parencite{faia2023energycommunity,srithapon2023predictive}. An agreed local price can make sharing beneficial to both households, giving the producer a better return than exporting the surplus and the consumer a lower cost than buying from the grid, after applicable charges \parencite{moret2019energycollectives,portugal2022electricity}. Matching demand to this production can also reduce upstream power flows and electrical losses \parencite{pohl2024distribution}, and make use of electricity that export limits would otherwise require to be curtailed \parencite{hodge2017charging}.

Realising these benefits requires attention to how members' decisions interact. If every \gls{ev} owner independently chooses the cheapest charging period, their combined demand may create a new peak in grid imports \parencite{hodge2017charging}. Spreading charging across the night can avoid this concentration, but must accommodate different departure times; for example, a vehicle leaving at 06:00 may need to charge sooner than one leaving at 08:00. Coordination therefore has to reconcile the \gls{rec}'s objective with each member's service requirements. Where participation is possible, this flexibility can also support \emph{\gls{dr}}, adjusting consumption in response to prices or incentives to serve needs beyond the \gls{rec} \parencite{eu2019electricity,erse2024firme}.

This need to reconcile individual requirements with shared objectives connects the household decisions in Section~\ref{sec:intro-energy-flexibility} to the multi-agent coordination introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro-marl}. In a \gls{marl} formulation with one agent per building, each agent manages its local resources while learning how its decisions contribute to the \gls{rec}'s objective. A charging decision can then account for the driver's needs together with the availability of another household's surplus or the demand created by other vehicles. Experience of these interactions provides a basis for learning coordinated behaviour, with surveys of simulated energy systems reporting promising reductions in costs and demand peaks \parencite{keren2024energynetworks,sajid2026marldrreview}.

\glspl{rec} therefore provide a setting for investigating the central problem of this thesis, which concerns putting \gls{marl} to use in the real world. Individual and shared objectives must be reconciled across members with service requirements and changing resource configurations. These and other challenges of applying \gls{marl} in real-world installations are examined in Section~\ref{sec:intro-problem}.

\section{Research Problem}
\label{sec:intro-problem}
\label{sec:intro-problem-statement}

Promising simulation results in robotics, traffic control, complex strategy games, and energy management have encouraged interest in the wider use of \gls{marl} \parencite{gronauer2022survey,orr2023multirobot,keren2024energynetworks}. Organisations seeking to bring these methods beyond simulation and into real-world applications need controllers that work with the equipment and information available, deliver the services people rely on, and remain useful as operating conditions change. Applying \gls{marl} under these conditions requires the learning methods and the systems supporting their operation to be developed together. The following subsections discuss the challenges involved and explain the choice and meaning of scalability and dynamic reconfiguration, deployability, safety, and robustness as the four dimensions that guide this thesis.

\subsection{Real-World Challenges}
\label{sec:intro-challenges}

Applying \gls{marl} in the real world involves several challenges identified in the literature but only partly examined in simulation studies. In energy management, reviews report more attention to costs and demand peaks than to robustness, transferability, and deployment conditions \parencite{keren2024energynetworks,sajid2026marldrreview}. These real-world challenges include scaling coordination to larger groups, adapting policies to different and changing resources, obtaining sufficient learning experience, reusing learned behaviour across installations, and connecting decisions to physical equipment \parencite{gronauer2022survey,orr2023multirobot,kannari2025realbuildings}. Respecting physical limits and individual service requirements while pursuing shared objectives, and maintaining control under disturbance, add further demands \parencite{gu2023safemarl,dulacarnold2021realworld}, alongside questions about fairness, explainability, privacy, and cybersecurity \parencite{moret2019energycollectives,bekkemoen2024xrl,farokhi2020smartmeterprivacy,stouffer2023otsecurity}. Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work} examines these gaps in detail. The discussion below summarises them to frame the research problem through three perspectives, (i) the practical perspective, concerning the organisations that must install, operate, and maintain controllers across different application domains, (ii) the \gls{rec} perspective, concerning their consequences for the members, equipment, and energy services introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro-rec}, and (iii) the scientific perspective, concerning \gls{marl} algorithms and their associated challenges.

\emph{Scalability and dynamic reconfiguration} become practical concerns when organisations extend coordinated control to more participants or adapt it to changing resources. Expanding a robot fleet, for example, increases the number of agents whose movements and tasks must be coordinated \parencite{orr2023multirobot}. The training time, memory, communication, and decision time required by \gls{marl} algorithms can limit how widely an organisation can apply them \parencite{charbonnier2022scalable}. From the \gls{rec} perspective, this difficulty appears in \glspl{rec} with different numbers of members and different combinations of resources, whose coordination must remain useful as the population grows \parencite{almilaify2023scalex}. These conditions raise scientific challenges for \gls{marl}. A larger population introduces more combinations of decisions, while each agent learns in an environment partly shaped by the changing behaviour of the others \parencite{gronauer2022survey,orr2023multirobot}. If a method uses a separately trained policy for each building, adding a member may also require training another policy and adapting coordination with the existing agents \parencite{wu2024dynamicvpp}.

\emph{Dynamic reconfiguration} requires control to adapt as resources, capabilities, and relationships change over time. In a \gls{rec}, this includes members joining or leaving, as introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro-rec}, and changes within a building even when the number of agents stays the same. A new charger gives its building's agent another service to manage, while removing a battery takes away actions that an earlier policy could use. The scientific difficulty lies in accommodating these changes within the learned model. If it expects a fixed collection of inputs and outputs, routine equipment changes may require modifying it and repeating part of the training. Generalisation to new actions is an established \gls{rl} problem, and energy studies examine representations for changing building populations and equipment configurations \parencite{jain2020newactions,wu2024dynamicvpp,orfanoudakis2026gnndt}.

\emph{Deployability} concerns the effort an organisation needs to put a learned controller into service and maintain it across installations. Obtaining suitable learning experience may be costly or difficult, and exploring new actions on physical equipment can interfere with services already being provided \parencite{dulacarnold2021realworld}. From the \gls{rec} perspective, a new member may have little usable historical data, while differences in equipment, demand, tariffs, and occupants' routines can limit the value of experience collected elsewhere. These practical conditions raise scientific questions about how much experience \gls{marl} algorithms require and how well learned behaviour generalises beyond its training conditions. Transfer studies in simulated building populations examine reuse of learned behaviour, while reviews of real building control identify generalisation between installations as an outstanding need \parencite{nweye2023merlin,kannari2025realbuildings}.

The practical demands of deployability also extend across the model's lifecycle, from collecting and preparing sensor data to training, validation, deployment, monitoring, maintenance, and updates \parencite{kreuzberger2023mlops,li2022rlops}. These stages must work together with manageable configuration and intervention effort. Integration with operational data and control systems is also needed in domains such as traffic management and telecommunications \parencite{zhang2025realworldvsl,li2022rlops}. In a \gls{rec}, the sensing and control architecture introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro-energy-flexibility} must obtain usable measurements from different devices, associate them with the right resources, and deliver commands to the equipment. Because installations differ, deploying a controller at a new site may require repeating data preparation, model validation, and device integration \parencite{kannari2025realbuildings,fonseca2025realworldrec}. For the \gls{marl} formulation, this raises the question of whether its assumed observations and actions retain their meaning when the policy operates through these interfaces.

\emph{Safety} requires operators to keep applied decisions within physical limits while preserving the services expected by users. In a robot fleet, coordinated movement must avoid collisions \parencite{orr2023multirobot}; in the \gls{rec} charging example, decisions must respect electrical limits while delivering the energy needed before departure. Representing the driver's request alongside the equipment's capabilities determines how much flexibility can actually be used \parencite{hussain2023flexibility,siksnys2019flexoffers}. For \gls{marl} algorithms, a reward may favour an economical schedule, but learning and execution must also account for actions that are physically inadmissible or leave too little time to complete charging. Safe \gls{marl} and shielding methods address how constraints enter learning and how proposed actions can be corrected before execution \parencite{gu2023safemarl,elsayedaly2021shielding}. The coordination problem also includes how those corrections affect other agents sharing the available capacity.

\Needspace{3\baselineskip}
\emph{Robustness} matters to organisations that need control to continue under disturbances and changing operating conditions, since interrupted service can require manual intervention. A mobile robot, for example, may have to continue navigating when its sensor readings become unreliable \parencite{dulacarnold2021realworld}. In a \gls{rec}, a missing measurement or failed charger removes information or actions on which a learned policy normally relies, while changes in occupants' routines can alter demand even when all equipment works correctly. Building-control reviews and operational \gls{rec} studies document the effects of data and interface problems \parencite{kannari2025realbuildings,fonseca2025realworldrec}. An unavailable charger may affect one household while other resources remain usable. From the scientific perspective, these situations require learned behaviour to remain useful with incomplete observations or changes in the effects of actions, including conditions outside the training experience \parencite{dulacarnold2021realworld}. Continued service then depends on how the policy responds and how the surrounding system recognises the disruption and maintains or restores control.

\emph{Fairness} concerns how the benefits and burdens of coordination are distributed among participants. In a \gls{rec}, a lower collective cost may be accompanied by unequal savings or repeated inconvenience for some members \parencite{moret2019energycollectives}. If the same household's charging is repeatedly postponed, participants and operators need to understand the reason and whether intervention is appropriate. For \gls{marl}, these concerns involve both how the shared reward encourages particular outcomes \parencite{albrecht2024marl} and how the resulting decisions can be interpreted, motivating research on \emph{explainability} \parencite{bekkemoen2024xrl}.

\emph{Privacy} and \emph{cybersecurity} require organisations to protect the information and interfaces used for coordination against unauthorised access or alteration, as in other operational control systems \parencite{stouffer2023otsecurity}. In a \gls{rec}, detailed energy measurements can reveal household routines, affecting what data can be made available for learning and exchanged during operation \parencite{farokhi2020smartmeterprivacy}. Protection also covers the links carrying measurements and equipment commands. From the scientific perspective, restrictions on information must be reflected in the observations and communication assumed by the \gls{marl} formulation.

\subsection{Lexical Grounding}
\label{sec:intro-lexicon}

The thesis examines scalability and dynamic reconfiguration, deployability, safety, and robustness as four dimensions of applying \gls{marl} in the real world. Their selection draws on the literature reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work} and experience of connecting learned controllers to real-world installations in the supporting research projects. These dimensions guide the \glspl{rq} about learning methods and their supporting systems; the definitions below establish their meaning.

\paragraph{Scalability and dynamic reconfiguration.}
In this thesis, scalability denotes the ability to sustain useful control across increasing numbers and diverse combinations of agents and resources, with manageable growth in computational demand \parencite{bondi2000scalability,charbonnier2022scalable}. Dynamic reconfiguration denotes adaptation to changes in membership, equipment, or coordination relationships during operation \parencite{wu2024dynamicvpp,orfanoudakis2026gnndt}. These terms jointly cover variation in \gls{rec} populations and in the resources within each building.

\paragraph{Deployability.}
Deployability is used here for the ability to introduce and maintain a learned controller in real-world installations with manageable preparation, training, adaptation, and integration effort. Its scope covers reuse across installations \parencite{nweye2023merlin} and the model lifecycle, from data collection and preparation to training, validation, deployment, monitoring, maintenance, and updates \parencite{li2022rlops,kreuzberger2023mlops}.

\paragraph{Safety.}
Safety denotes compliance with the physical, electrical, and service constraints specified in this thesis, for both applied actions and resulting states \parencite{gu2023safemarl,elsayedaly2021shielding}. These constraints cover equipment and connection limits and service commitments such as delivering the required energy before departure.

\paragraph{Robustness.}
Robustness refers here to retaining acceptable control performance and service under disturbances and conditions outside the training experience \parencite{dulacarnold2021realworld}. Its scope covers changing operating patterns, sensor and data faults, communication delays or losses, unavailable equipment, and execution failures. The related term \emph{resilience} denotes the capacity to recover useful operation after a disruption.

\endgroup

These dimensions are closely related, and improvements in one can also benefit another. Greater scalability, for example, can make a system easier to apply in installations of different sizes, contributing to deployability. The thesis therefore considers these dimensions together when assessing the suitability of \gls{marl} for real-world control.

\section{Thesis Statement and Research Questions}
\label{sec:intro-rqs}

The challenges discussed in Section~\ref{sec:intro-problem} lead to the thesis statement and \glspl{rq} presented below. Together, these guide the investigation of how \gls{marl} can be applied in the real world.

\Needspace{12\baselineskip}
\subsection{Thesis Statement}
\label{sec:intro-statement}

The central statement examined in this thesis is:

\begin{quote}
\emph{\gls{marl} can support scalable, deployable, safe, and robust control of \glspl{rec} through the integration of reusable learning across compatible resources, explicit constraint handling, and execution supervision.}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:intro-questions}

The four \glspl{rq} examine this proposition along the dimensions defined in Subsection~\ref{sec:intro-lexicon}.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.65\baselineskip,parsep=0pt,topsep=0.5\baselineskip]
\item \textbf{\gls{rq} 1: Scalability and Dynamic Reconfiguration} -- How can \gls{marl} accommodate increasing populations and changes in agents and local resources, and how do these changes affect control performance, computational demand, and the need for further learning?

\item \textbf{\gls{rq} 2: Deployability of Learned Controllers} -- Which parts of controller preparation can be reused across heterogeneous installations, and what training, adaptation, integration, and maintenance effort is required at each installation to retain control performance?

\item \textbf{\gls{rq} 3: Physical and Operational Safety} -- How can physical and service constraints be represented and enforced in \gls{marl}-based control, and how does their enforcement affect coordination, individual service delivery, and the need for protective intervention?

\item \textbf{\gls{rq} 4: Robustness to Real-World Disturbances} -- How do learned policies and execution supervision contribute to service continuity and sustained recovery under changing operating conditions and failures in data, communication, or equipment?
\end{itemize}

These questions are addressed through comparisons of energy performance and service delivery alongside the computation, preparation, and intervention required to obtain them. Reconfiguration studies distinguish immediate policy reuse from performance obtained after further learning. Deployability studies separate preparation shared across installations from work repeated at each site. Safety and robustness assessments follow individual services through protective intervention, degraded operation, and recovery, accounting for the feasibility of the requested service under the available resources. The evaluation methodology in Chapter~\ref{chap:simulator} defines the corresponding indicators and comparison conditions.

\section{Research Contributions and Outcomes}
\label{sec:intro-contributions}

This research advances the study and real-world application of \gls{marl} through contributions to learning methods, control architectures, simulation, and experimental evaluation. Developed in \gls{rec} energy management, the work connects the coordination of heterogeneous resources with the conditions required to apply learned decisions in practice. Subsection~\ref{sec:intro-scientific-contributions} presents the scientific contributions, Subsection~\ref{sec:intro-software-contributions} describes the software and data outputs, and Subsection~\ref{sec:intro-societal} discusses the societal and environmental relevance of the work.

\subsection{Scientific Contributions}
\label{sec:intro-scientific-contributions}

The \acrlongpl{sc} (\acrshort{sc})\glsunset{sc} developed in this thesis concern the analysis of existing research, the organisation and evaluation of control, and learning under changing configurations and operating conditions.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.6em,labelwidth=3em,labelsep=0.6em,align=left,font=\normalfont\bfseries,itemsep=0.55\baselineskip,parsep=0pt,topsep=0.5\baselineskip]
\item[SC1] \textbf{A review and gap analysis of \gls{marl} for real-world control.}\par\nobreak
A systematised review connects evidence from \gls{rec} applications with methods addressing scalability and dynamic reconfiguration, deployability, safety, and robustness. Its comparative synthesis identifies demonstrated capabilities, limitations in their evaluation, and unresolved questions that guide the investigation (Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}).

\item[SC2] \textbf{An architecture for multi-agent \gls{rec} control.}\par\nobreak
This thesis presents EnergAIze, a distributed architecture that connects controller development, \gls{rec} management, and supervised operation. EnergAIze separates control decisions from installation-specific data and equipment interfaces, supporting heterogeneous installations through common descriptions of resources and services. Its modular organisation connects local control with shared data, deployment, and monitoring services (Chapter~\ref{chap:energAIze}).

\item[SC3] \textbf{A simulation model for changing \gls{rec} configurations and services.}\par\nobreak
This thesis presents CityLearn v3, a simulation environment that represents \glspl{rec}, their members, energy assets, and service requests. The model connects changes in resource relationships and availability to energy exchanges, electrical constraints, and service delivery, supporting controlled studies of reconfiguration and disturbances before real-world application (Chapter~\ref{chap:simulator}).

\item[SC4] \textbf{A methodology for comparing learned \gls{rec} controllers.}\par\nobreak
This thesis proposes a set of \glspl{kpi} and a methodology for comparing controllers during development and real-world application. The assessment combines energy performance, service delivery, computational demand, and protective interventions to identify regressions and operational difficulties. Common definitions support comparisons between learned controllers and other control approaches (Section~\ref{sec:sim-evaluation}).

\item[SC5] \textbf{A formal model for \gls{marl} under dynamic reconfiguration.}\par\nobreak
This thesis presents a Markov-game formulation that links changes in agent participation, resource capabilities, and information availability to the observations and actions available to each agent. It distinguishes persistent equipment from temporary service requests and explicitly associates observations, actions, and constraints with the resources they concern. This formulation provides a common description of reconfiguration for learning and execution (Section~\ref{sec:timarl-decision-processes}).

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
\item[SC6] \textbf{A modular learning method for reconfigurable multi-agent control.}\par\nobreak
Building on SC5, this thesis presents \gls{timarl}, which uses the current resource description to construct each agent's decision interface and jointly select actions for its active equipment. The method supports investigation of policy reuse under combined changes in community membership and local equipment (Sections~\ref{sec:runtime-interface-model}--\ref{sec:tip-and-learning}).

\item[SC7] \textbf{Safety and robustness for real-world multi-agent control.}\par\nobreak
This thesis extends \gls{timarl} by propagating changes in equipment and information availability through declared dependencies to determine which control capabilities remain usable. Affected actions can be restricted or suspended while other resources continue under their own operating conditions, with explicit conditions governing their return (Section~\ref{sec:tcg-safety}).

\item[SC8] \textbf{An experimental study of strategies for improving learned \gls{rec} control.}\par\nobreak
This thesis investigates hierarchical coordination, offline \gls{rl}, \gls{bc}, and the use of operating rules to guide learning through matched experiments. The comparisons examine their effects on learning effort, energy performance, and service delivery, identifying the conditions in which each strategy can support controller development (Sections~\ref{sec:data-efficient-hierarchy}--\ref{sec:hrl}).

\item[SC9] \textbf{Application of learned control to heterogeneous real-world \glspl{rec}.}\par\nobreak
This thesis applies EnergAIze and its controllers to heterogeneous real-world \gls{rec} installations, connecting learned decisions to operational data and equipment interfaces. Integration and operational studies examine service delivery, supervision, and the effects of imperfect data and equipment availability, establishing practical requirements for applying the developed methods (Chapter~\ref{chap:real-world}).

\item[SC10] \textbf{Scientific publications.}\par\nobreak
The publications associated with these contributions include nine published papers, comprising five conference papers and four journal articles. These works, together with a further conference communication, submitted manuscripts, preprints and planned articles, are listed in the front matter (p.~\pageref{sec:thesis-publications}).
\end{description}

\subsection{Societal and Environmental Relevance}
\label{sec:intro-societal}

The thesis supports more efficient energy management by developing methods to coordinate local generation, storage, and flexible demand. In \glspl{rec}, this coordination creates opportunities to use more locally produced renewable electricity, reduce energy expenditure, and manage demand peaks while preserving comfort and mobility \parencite{reis2021businessmodels,hussain2023flexibility}. Its environmental relevance lies in making distributed flexibility usable in everyday operation, supporting renewable integration and the transition towards low-carbon energy systems.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
The societal contribution centres on the services that energy systems provide to people. Household needs and the distribution of benefits among \gls{rec} members remain explicit considerations in control and evaluation \parencite{moret2019energycollectives}. Open software, reusable datasets, and documented methods extend the value of the work to researchers and organisations developing their own energy-management solutions. Together, these contributions support the United Nations \glspl{sdg} of affordable and clean energy (\gls{sdg}~7), sustainable cities and communities (\gls{sdg}~11), and climate action (\gls{sdg}~13) \parencite{un2015agenda2030}.

\section{Thesis Organisation}
\label{sec:intro-approach}
\label{sec:intro-graphical-abstract}
\label{sec:intro-objectives}
\label{sec:intro-organisation}

The thesis is organised as a connected investigation of the conditions required to apply \gls{marl} in real-world systems.

Chapter~\ref{chap:foundations} establishes the concepts of energy flexibility, \glspl{rec}, \gls{rl}, and policy organisation used throughout the thesis. Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work} compares existing control approaches and develops the research gaps that guide the subsequent chapters.

Chapter~\ref{chap:energAIze} presents the system architecture (EnergAIze), explaining how learning algorithms fit alongside the data, deployment, and supervision services needed for practical control. Chapter~\ref{chap:simulator} then establishes the experimental environment in which control decisions can be studied under controlled, comparable conditions. This includes the simulator extensions developed throughout the research, the datasets, and the \glspl{kpi} used to evaluate controllers.

On this basis, Chapter~\ref{chap:control-benchmark} investigates the methods identified in the state-of-the-art review, examining their capabilities and the requirements that remain unresolved. These studies motivate the method presented and evaluated in Chapter~\ref{chap:ti-marl}, which brings policy reuse, coordination, and operational constraints into an integrated design.

Chapter~\ref{chap:real-world} follows with deployment and operational studies in real-world installations. These studies examine the practical implications of the earlier chapters, including the effects of data availability, equipment interfaces, and operating conditions on controller integration and use.

Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion} draws together the findings to answer the \glspl{rq}. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusions} assesses the central thesis, summarises the contributions, and outlines future work. The appendices provide the detailed literature comparison and supporting experimental material.
