\chapter{Conclusions and Future Research}
\label{chap:conclusions}
\markboth{Conclusions and Future Research}{Conclusions and Future Research}

This thesis investigated how \gls{marl} can move from controlled experiments towards use in distributed, heterogeneous, and dynamically reconfigurable \glspl{cps}. \glspl{rec} and \gls{ev} charging provided a demanding empirical setting: local assets interact through shared infrastructure; participants expose different information and flexibility; chargers, vehicles, and sessions change independently; physical and service limits cannot be ignored; and the controller must eventually run through real software, devices, and organisations.

The research did not produce one isolated algorithm followed by an application. It produced a connected path. EnergAIze defined the architecture and responsibilities. The simulator made the relevant conditions reproducible. A progression of controllers established which problems could be solved with transparent rules, explicit optimisation, single-agent learning, conventional \gls{marl}, data-efficient learning, and hierarchy. \gls{timarl} addressed the remaining fixed-interface assumption. Experiment orchestration and distributed inference carried controller artefacts across heterogeneous computing environments. \gls{opeva} and DemFlex then provided distinct real-world contexts for testing the resulting claims.

\section{What the Research Established}
\label{sec:conclusion-recap}
\label{sec:conclusion-findings}

The state-of-the-art review showed that scalable \gls{marl}, dynamic teams, safe learning, physical testbeds, deployment tooling, and long-running intelligent systems already contain strong individual solutions. The residual problem was their composition. The assumptions that make one mechanism effective often remain implicit and can fail when the system interface, physical constraints, population, information quality, or runtime changes.

The first conclusion is that the experimental world is part of the method. The simulator is not a neutral source of reward. Its identities, timebase, units, assets, events, constraints, observations, actions, settlement, and data-quality rules determine what a controller can learn and what a result means. The annual dataset suite and the CityLearn v3 extensions make heterogeneous members, multiple chargers, \gls{ev} sessions, deferrable services, phase limits, health problems, and topology changes available under repeatable conditions. This foundation permits the later algorithmic claims to be tested instead of assumed.

The second conclusion is that controller complexity must be justified by the control problem. Rule-based control remains valuable for transparency, feasibility, development, and fallback. Optimisation remains valuable when the model and horizon are available and an explicit bound or operational schedule is needed. Single-agent methods isolate adaptation from coordination. Conventional \gls{marl} becomes useful when distributed information and repeated interaction matter. \gls{matd3}, guided exploration, \gls{bc}, replay strategies, offline learning, distillation, and hierarchy address specific problems of stability, data use, runtime cost, and local--\gls{rec} decomposition. Their value does not disappear when \gls{timarl} is introduced.

The third conclusion concerns the object presented to the policy. Fixed observation and action arrays are appropriate while all relevant entities and meanings are known in advance. They become fragile when one building gains a second charger, an \gls{ev} session moves, a device loses a capability, or a measurement failure changes which action remains defensible. \gls{timarl} represents the active local interface as a versioned, entity-bound object. A deterministic compiler derives typed observations, dynamic groups, health-dependent validity, bounds, dependencies, and local constraints. A shared actor produces one coherent hybrid bundle for the groups that currently exist. \gls{tippo} and \gls{timappo} use the same actor and execution path, differing in whether value estimation is local or conditioned on the active population.

The fourth conclusion is that learned actions and feasible actions must remain distinguishable. The actor produces a raw bundle; the analytic projector applies local service and electrical requirements; the device or simulator returns the applied outcome. Keeping these stages in the trace reveals whether good physical behaviour came from the policy, the feasibility layer, external protection, or a combination. The current guarantee is local and model-dependent. Shared limits remain an empirical multi-agent outcome because the evaluated \gls{timarl} version contains no cross-member hard corrector.

The fifth conclusion is that scale has several independent costs. Parameter sharing can keep model size stable while token processing, critic work, communication, simulation, or sample demand grows. Distributed workers can shorten a campaign without making the algorithm itself more scalable. A policy can train on a \gls{gpu} and still fail an edge latency or compatibility requirement. Population, heterogeneity, topology events, memory, throughput, communication, training time, inference time, and control quality must be reported together.

The sixth conclusion is that deployment starts before a model file is copied and continues after it is loaded. The experiment, source, image, simulator, dataset, interface, reward, seed, checkpoint, package, target, active version, and observed actions form one evidence chain. The implemented lifecycle reconstructs jobs across heterogeneous workers, validates general inference bundles, loads selected versions into target-specific services, exposes health and logs, and permits operator-controlled reversal. Automatic coordinated rollout, \gls{kpi}-triggered rollback, and production \gls{timarl} serving remain separate engineering and research steps.

Finally, the real-world work shows why these distinctions matter. \gls{opeva} connects real participants, corporate charging, local controllers, external platforms, vehicle and flexibility information, historical data, simulation, and bounded actuation. Its reported studies show improvements in cost, peak, grid demand, ramping, and renewable use within their declared settings. They also expose missing \gls{soc}, asynchronous data, platform boundaries, site limits, partial scenario implementation, and heterogeneous participant outcomes. DemFlex supplies a second demonstrator whose claims must be derived from its own site, controller, data, authority, and duration.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:conclusion-rqs}

\paragraph{RQ1: scalability of heterogeneous multi-agent control.}
Changing participants and assets are handled by separating stable agent identity from module, entity, and session identity and by compiling the active control interface at runtime. Known observation and action semantics are reused across new instances, while validity determines which groups can act. A shared local actor preserves decentralised execution; aggregate context, coupled outcomes, and the \gls{timappo} set critic support \gls{rec} coordination. Parameter sharing, hierarchical typed encoding, dynamic group heads, a variable-population critic, and cached preparation remove several fixed-size assumptions without making computation independent of population or interface size. The answer is the measured envelope that satisfies both control and resource gates. Unknown semantic families still require explicit admission or fallback.

\paragraph{RQ2: deployability and governed MARL lifecycle.}
Reproducible job identity, heterogeneous worker adapters, persistent remote state, validated policy bundles, target profiles, inference health, active-version records, logs, and operator-controlled reversal connect training and retraining to serving. \gls{timarl} adds resolved interface, compiler, compatibility, persistence, and trace contracts to that hand-off. The resulting evidence concerns reconstruction, portability, compatibility, automation, traceability, time and manual intervention, and recoverable target state. Coordinated atomic rollout, automated promotion and rollback, and production \gls{timarl} serving remain beyond the current implementation evidence.

\paragraph{RQ3: physical and operational safety.}
Safety is incorporated through explicit scenario constraints, dynamic validity and bounds, local analytic projection, service floors, external supervision, device protection, and requested--projected--applied traces. The same physical, electrical, and service semantics connect training, evaluation, correction, and execution. The thesis supports conditional local feasibility and empirical shared-constraint assessment, not universal formal certification.

\paragraph{RQ4: robustness to real-world disturbances.}
Robustness is addressed through explicit fault scenarios, causal health and dependency rules, forced degraded modes, scoped fallback, recovery hysteresis, and traces that connect disturbances to retained service and applied action. Data, communication, equipment, and operating deviations are evaluated by type, severity, degradation, unnecessary service loss, intervention, and recovery. The answer remains conditional on the tested disturbance envelope and the correctness of the declared health and dependency models.

\section{Principal Contributions}
\label{sec:conclusion-contributions}

\gls{timarl} is the principal algorithmic contribution. Its novelty lies in the executable connection between a versioned, entity-bound control contract and multi-agent learning. The same typed semantics organise what the actor observes and invokes, how health changes validity, how local feasibility is applied, how topology-aware state is restored, and how decisions are traced. This contribution is narrower and more defensible than claiming novelty for \gls{ppo}, attention, parameter sharing, or action projection separately.

Six supporting contributions make that method meaningful and testable:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item an integrated formulation of real-world \gls{marl} applicability that connects control semantics, the four cross-cutting requirements, and explicit levels of evidence;
  \item the EnergAIze control and experimentation architecture, which assigns responsibilities and contracts across data, simulation, control, experimentation, inference, deployment, and operation;
  \item CityLearn v3 and the canonical annual \gls{rec} dataset suite for heterogeneous, event-driven, constrained, reconfigurable, and disturbance-aware experiments;
  \item a progressive family of rule-based, optimisation, single-agent, multi-agent, data-reuse, and hierarchical controllers evaluated under a common protocol;
  \item a governed experiment-to-inference lifecycle spanning heterogeneous compute, reproducible jobs and artefacts, bundle validation, target activation, monitoring, and operator-controlled reversal; and
  \item cross-level empirical evidence from simulation, real measurements, external platforms, edge execution, \gls{opeva}, and DemFlex, kept separate by demonstrator and evidence level.
\end{enumerate}

The software produced during the research includes the simulator, datasets and validation tools, controller implementations, \gls{timarl} compiler and runtime, orchestration services, worker adapters, inference service, backend and \gls{ui} support, experiment configurations, and demonstrator integrations. Software volume is evidence of engineering effort, not scientific validity. Tests, comparisons, ablations, systems measurements, releases, and demonstrator records provide the corresponding evidence.

\section{Assessment of the Central Statement}
\label{sec:conclusion-assessment}

The central statement evaluated by this dissertation was

\begin{quote}
\emph{\gls{marl} can improve energy performance in real-world \glspl{rec} while meeting participants' service requirements, with manageable computational and integration effort. Integrating learning, constraint handling, and supervised execution can preserve useful control as resources and operating conditions change.}
\end{quote}

The body of work supports that statement within the evaluated energy-domain scope. No individual layer carries the complete claim. A learning result depends on the world and interface against which it was produced. A feasible action depends on the current entities, measurements, bounds, and physical model. A checkpoint remains meaningful only while its preprocessing, compatibility, and execution contracts are preserved. A healthy process does not prove acceptable control, and a successful physical command does not prove seasonal benefit. The integrated design is necessary because a change at one layer can invalidate evidence obtained at another.

The statement is supported most strongly as a systems and method argument, with different empirical depths across its components. The simulator, progressive controllers, \gls{timarl} implementation, lifecycle, and \gls{opeva} evidence establish concrete parts of the path. Final multi-seed comparisons, complete cross-target systems measurements, production \gls{timarl} serving, DemFlex evidence, and long-duration field operation determine the remaining quantitative reach. This boundary narrows the conclusion without weakening its central lesson.

\section{Limits of the Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion-limits}

The empirical evidence is concentrated in \glspl{rec}, buildings, batteries, \gls{pv} systems, \glspl{ev}, and charging infrastructure. Mechanism-level transfer to another \gls{cps} is plausible where agents have changing known capabilities, entity-bound observations and actions, local and shared constraints, and heterogeneous runtime targets. Performance and safety do not transfer without new domain models and evidence.

The evaluated scenarios cover a wider range of topology, faults, physical pressure, and \gls{rec} size than the original benchmarks, but they remain designed scenarios. Field evidence is bounded by sites, duration, authority, participants, weather, platform access, and manual intervention. The local projector depends on the correctness of the encoded constraints. The central set critic uses a deliberately modest \gls{rec} summary. Unknown asset families, formal shared safety, adversarial security, certification, and fully autonomous fleet-wide updates remain outside the demonstrated contribution.

\section{Future Research Directions}
\label{sec:conclusion-future}

\paragraph{Admission of new asset families.}
A stronger plug-and-control process should identify a new asset, verify its schema and physical model, select a compatible policy or fallback, test local and shared constraints, and monitor its effect after admission. This requires governance for schema evolution as well as more asset types.

\paragraph{Shared physical assurance.}
Local projection should be complemented by allocation methods for shared feeder, transformer, or \gls{rec} constraints. The allocation rule must expose its consequences for service and fairness; a central corrector is not neutral merely because it restores feasibility.

\paragraph{Larger and richer TI-MARL structures.}
Future work should compare \gls{tippo} and \gls{timappo} with graph and attention-based critics, alternative learning backbones, larger populations, multiple \glspl{rec}, and richer electrical topology. These experiments must separate gains from the typed interface from gains produced by the optimiser or critic.

\paragraph{Assurance under model and data error.}
The compiler and projector should represent confidence in measurements and physical relations, detect inconsistent assumptions, and report when a guarantee no longer applies. Formal barrier, reachability, or shield methods are relevant where suitable models exist, but must also adapt to interface change.

\paragraph{Continual and distributed learning.}
Historical, offline, federated, and continual learning can reduce direct exploration and support adaptation across \glspl{rec}. Promotion gates, replayable evidence, privacy constraints, and protection against forgetting must remain part of the update process.

\paragraph{Coordinated deployment over intermittent networks.}
Mixed versions, partial activation, disconnection, local rollback, and recovery need explicit policy-set semantics. The next lifecycle step is to determine which combinations remain admissible while independent targets change at different times.

\paragraph{Long-duration and replicated real-world studies.}
Seasonal and multi-site studies should report energy and cost together with service, participant benefit, data incidents, projection and fallback interventions, rejected commands, resource use, policy changes, and operator actions. Such records are needed to distinguish an effective policy from an effective surrounding assurance system.

\paragraph{Fairness, explanation, security, and governance.}
Participant-level benefit, explanation of binding and projection decisions, human override, privacy, cybersecurity, certification, and regulatory accountability deserve dedicated research beyond their role as constraints in this thesis.

\section{Closing Remarks}

The work began with learning-based energy coordination and expanded as real systems exposed assumptions that a nominal benchmark could leave invisible. Feasible control led to explicit optimisation. Adaptation led to decentralised coordination. Training instability and data scarcity led to stronger learning and reuse mechanisms. Local and \gls{rec} objectives led to hierarchy. Changes in assets, sessions, health, and action meaning led to \gls{timarl}. Reproducibility and real-world use led to orchestration, packaging, inference, monitoring, and recovery.

The final lesson is simple but demanding. A credible intelligent controller must expose what it controls, what it can currently observe and invoke, how an action becomes feasible, which experiment produced the policy, which runtime is executing it, what was actually applied, and what evidence supports its continued use. EnergAIze supplies the surrounding system, \gls{timarl} supplies a policy formulation for changing typed interfaces, and the simulator, controller progression, lifecycle, and demonstrators supply the evidence needed to test both. Together, they move the discussion from a successful learning curve towards an auditable path for intelligent control in changing physical systems.
