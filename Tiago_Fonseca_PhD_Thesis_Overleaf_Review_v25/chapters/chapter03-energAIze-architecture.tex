\chapter{EnergAIze: An Integrated Framework for Intelligent Energy Management}
\label{chap:energAIze}
\markboth{The EnergAIze Framework}{The EnergAIze Framework}

Control of a Renewable Energy Community extends well beyond the algorithm that selects an action. Before a controller can decide when an electric vehicle should charge, it must receive coherent measurements from different platforms, estimate information that is not directly observable, understand the user's flexibility, and translate its decision into a command accepted by the physical device. The same path must remain useful when measurements are delayed, a vehicle does not expose its state of charge, an asset becomes unavailable, or an external platform rejects an action. Data integration, prediction, optimisation, learning, safety, and deployment form one control system in a real community.

EnergAIze is the integrated framework developed in this thesis to connect those concerns. It turns heterogeneous energy and mobility data into supervised decisions for flexible assets while preserving a controlled route for simulation, learning, evaluation, packaging, and runtime execution. Percepta and the Data Provider establish the integration boundary; prediction and vehicle services complete the controller's view of each installation; the Algorithms component produces local and community-aware decisions; the Fail-Safe Supervisor preserves physical and user constraints; CityLearn and EVLearn provide the experimental environment; and the inference and lifecycle services carry selected controllers towards real execution \parencite{fonseca2024energAIze,fonseca2025evlearn,fonseca2025realworldrec,percepta2025,opeva2026d63}.

The architecture is easiest to understand by widening the scope progressively. A single charging decision first exposes the information, safety, robustness, and execution requirements that exist even inside one building. Repeating that decision across heterogeneous participants then exposes the scale, coordination, and deployment problem. Only after these pressures are visible is the detailed component architecture introduced. This progression follows the way the research problem emerged in practice: \gls{scalability}, \gls{deployability}, \gls{safety}, and \gls{robustness} arise from the control setting and are visible before the software diagram is complete.

\section{Architectural View I: A Supervised Energy Decision}
\label{sec:energAIze-overview}
\label{sec:energaize-overview}

Consider one decision for an EV connected to a prosumer building. The site meter and photovoltaic inverter may report through an energy-management platform, the charger through a charging-service platform, and the vehicle through a manufacturer-facing service. Their messages use different identifiers, units, sampling periods, and communication mechanisms. Consumption and photovoltaic production over the decision horizon are uncertain forecasts. The intended departure time and minimum departure energy can originate in a recurrent routine, a prediction, or an explicit user request. Before any optimisation is attempted, the controller must know what each value means, which physical entity it describes, when it was produced, and how much confidence can be placed in it.

The resulting installation state is not yet an action. A controller interprets that state together with prices, objectives, flexibility windows, and current constraints and proposes a charging, discharging, storage, or deferral decision. A supervision stage then checks whether the proposal remains admissible for the connected asset and the installation. Only the supervised result is translated into the protocol and command expected by the external platform. Telemetry and the provider response close the decision loop, allowing the system to distinguish what was proposed, what was requested, and what the device actually executed.

Three cross-cutting requirements are already unavoidable in this single decision. \emph{Robustness} is required because a number in an observation does not reveal whether it is measured, forecast, stale, substituted, or unavailable. The appropriate response can range from normal inference to a contracted action range or conservative fallback. \emph{Safety} is required because state-of-charge limits, charger power, site capacity, connection state, and the user's departure requirement cannot be traded freely against cost or community reward. They influence state construction, the controller formulation, and the final supervision of the command.

\emph{Deployability} appears as soon as the action must leave the model. The controller must execute within the decision interval, reconstruct the same preprocessing used during training, produce a named and bounded action, and communicate it through an interface that the provider and device accept. An action that is optimal in a notebook but cannot be interpreted, executed, or traced at the installation is not a usable energy decision.

Scalability remains mostly latent at this first view. The loop concerns one installation and one decision deadline. Its importance becomes visible when the same semantic and execution contract must be preserved across participants whose assets, objectives, interfaces, connectivity, and computing resources are not identical.

\section{Architectural View II: A Distributed Renewable Energy Community}
\label{sec:energaize-community-view}

Now consider the same decision loop across a Renewable Energy Community. One participant may contribute only demand and photovoltaic measurements. Another may expose a stationary battery and one domestic charger. A household can have two chargers whose sessions and actions vary independently. An office or service building can host several chargers under one site limit, while a larger installation can add local controllers, three-phase limits, and different actuation authority. Treating these participants as copies of one fixed vector would hide precisely the heterogeneity that determines what each local controller can observe and do.

EnergAIze uses a repeatable installation-level control boundary whose contents are configured by the assets and information available at that site. Local observations, user requirements, asset constraints, and physical actions remain associated with their installation. Community coordination contributes only the shared context required by the selected control formulation, such as a price, aggregate exchange, peak, ramping signal, demand-response request, or authorised message from other agents. This keeps local physical decisions identifiable within the community objective.

\Cref{fig:energaize-community-view} widens the first view. Each installation retains the same semantic loop, but its asset composition and execution environment differ. The community layer connects these loops while preserving their local differences.

At this scope, \emph{scalability} is no longer equivalent to adding buildings. Population increases the number of decisions, but heterogeneity also increases the number and meaning of observations and action groups. Several chargers inside one building increase local action coupling without increasing the agent count. Community coordination introduces communication and aggregation costs. Training adds simulator throughput, replay, model, and experiment-management demands. Deployment adds memory, latency, image, network, and target-management requirements. The architecture must allow these dimensions to be measured separately because improving one does not guarantee improvement in the others.

Deployability also becomes a distributed-systems concern. One installation can execute centrally, another on a server-side container, and another near its assets on an edge device. Training can use a personal workstation, an institutional server, an HPC system, or a remote GPU platform whose runtime, storage, authentication, and image requirements differ. A common experiment and policy contract is needed so that changing the execution target does not silently change the scientific meaning of the run or the runtime meaning of the controller.

Safety and robustness become coupled across levels. A local charger action is constrained by its EV and circuit, while several chargers can compete for one site limit and several buildings can contribute to a shared grid condition. A participant can leave, an EV session can end, an asset can fail, or a community message can become stale. Local control should continue where its information and authority remain sufficient. Shared coordination must expose its degraded state and prevent obsolete context from producing an apparently valid action.

The two architectural views make algorithm selection only one part of the research problem. The control method operates inside a system that must preserve meaning across heterogeneous participants, increasing scale, physical constraints, degraded information, and different training and deployment environments.

\subsection{Percepta and the Data Provider}
\label{sec:energaize-percepta}

Percepta addresses the first obstacle to intelligent control: physical installations do not produce model-ready observations. Cleanwatts, my.i-charging, meters, local controllers, vehicle services, and other IoT providers expose different APIs, message formats, identifiers, units, and sampling rates. Coupling a controller directly to those details would make each provider or asset change a modification of the decision model. Percepta provides the stream-processing capability that separates transport and provider semantics from the installation-level representation \parencite{percepta2025}.

In the OPEVA instantiation, the Data Provider middleware realises this boundary for both historical-data preparation and runtime inference \parencite{opeva2026d63}. A receiver contains the communication logic for a source, such as HTTP for a REST service or RabbitMQ for a message-based provider. A translator extracts the relevant fields and converts the provider message into the common EnergAIze representation. Installation queues and an accumulator decouple subsequent processing from the original source. A manager then aligns the decision interval, checks missing values, reconstructs relationships among assets, and assembles the observation expected by the controller.

This organisation makes a new provider a bounded integration task: its receiver and translator can be added without modifying the other providers or the controller. It also keeps data treatment visible. Resampling a power measurement, interpolating a short gap, repeating a previous state, and substituting a forecast are not equivalent operations. The source, timestamp, quality, and transformation of a value are part of the information needed to determine whether normal control remains defensible.

The historical-data path retrieves and aligns installation records for simulation and training. The runtime path performs the same semantic work under a decision deadline, constructs an inference request, receives a supervised decision, and forwards the provider-specific request. Percepta and the Data Provider are bidirectional: they carry observations towards the controller and validated device requests towards the responsible external platform.

The OPEVA middleware calls the service that invokes an installation's AI model the \emph{Predictor}. In this specific interface, the term denotes an inference gateway and does not refer to a forecasting model. Throughout the thesis, runtime inference executes a control policy, whereas prediction estimates future or unavailable quantities used by that policy.

\subsection{Prediction, PulseCharge, and User Flexibility}
\label{sec:energaize-prediction}

A useful scheduling decision requires information that is not always measured directly. Near-term consumption and photovoltaic production influence whether flexible demand should be served immediately or deferred. A charger may report connection and delivered energy without exposing the EV state of charge. The user's departure time and required energy are behavioural service constraints, separate from electrical telemetry. EnergAIze represents each source explicitly and retains its meaning outside the numerical observation vector.

Consumption and photovoltaic-production services estimate the exogenous profiles used by the scheduling controller. Their outputs permit anticipation of a demand peak or local photovoltaic surplus and can provide a bounded substitute during a temporary sensor interruption. A forecast remains identifiable as a forecast, allowing the same controller to be evaluated with perfect, predicted, degraded, or unavailable information. Forecast models can evolve independently of provider adapters, safety supervision, and the control method.

PulseCharge supplies vehicle context that the charging infrastructure alone often cannot provide. It hides manufacturer-specific authentication and telemetry behind a common vehicle-data interface and retrieves state of charge, connection, and charging state where available. Request budgets and periodic-task supervision reflect the rate limits and temporary failures of manufacturer-facing services.

The associated flexibility model orders information by authority and confidence. A non-integrated or uncontrollable vehicle exposes no actionable flexibility. A recurrent schedule provides a low-interaction baseline. Flexibility Prediction estimates likely connection windows and energy needs from historical behaviour. Explicit user input takes precedence when the current journey differs from the routine. This hierarchy prevents a statistically convenient prediction from silently overriding a declared departure requirement \parencite{opeva2026d63}.

The Route Planner extends the same information model to an EV in transit by combining route, range, charger, price, and community information. It is an optional application component; installation-level control can operate without it. The evidence for its integration is bounded separately in \cref{chap:real-world}.

\subsection{Algorithms, Inference, and the Fail-Safe Supervisor}
\label{sec:energaize-algorithms}

The Algorithms component is the decision-making core of EnergAIze. In the OPEVA demonstrator it is also described as the Flexibility Scheduling Algorithm. It receives the installation state assembled by the Data Provider, including measurements, prices, forecasts, asset availability, vehicle requirements, and the community information permitted by the selected formulation. It returns named actions such as a battery charge or discharge request, an EV charging limit, or the schedule of a deferrable load \parencite{fonseca2024energAIze,opeva2026d63}.

Each installation controller represents local objectives and available assets. Community coordination introduces effects that cannot be inferred from one local meter alone, including aggregate peak, renewable sharing, ramping, and a common demand-response request. EnergAIze is not tied to one algorithm family: rule-based control provides an interpretable baseline and fallback, optimisation exposes explicit objectives and constraints, reinforcement learning supports adaptive local control, and MARL or hierarchical methods coordinate several participants. TI-MARL later addresses the fixed-interface assumptions that remain when assets, agents, and capabilities change. Their formulations and experimental evidence belong to Chapters~\ref{chap:control-benchmark} and~\ref{chap:ti-marl}; this chapter treats every method through its declared observation and action contract.

The energAIze inference runtime preserves that contract outside the training process. A deployable bundle associates policy artefacts with observation names, encoders, action names, action bounds, topology, and deployment metadata. The runtime reconstructs preprocessing and executes either a learned model or a deterministic policy behind the same request interface. Explicit aliases keep transport-specific field names outside the policy. Chapter~\ref{chap:lifecycle} describes the bundle, runtime, and deployment mechanisms in detail.

The result of inference is a proposed action, not unconditional physical authority. The Fail-Safe Supervisor checks it against rated power, state-of-charge bounds, connection state, vehicle departure requirements, site and phase limits, unavailable observations, and the authority granted to the current controller. According to the installation and failure condition, it can bound or reject the action, retain a previously safe value, or invoke a conservative rule-based alternative. External platforms, local controllers, and device firmware retain their lower-level protection and can still reject the resulting command.

EnergAIze records the observation context, controller and model version, proposed action, supervised request, provider response, executed value where available, and relevant KPIs. These records separate policy behaviour from the protection applied around it. A stable physical trajectory accompanied by frequent supervisor corrections demonstrates an effective protection layer, but it does not by itself demonstrate a reliable learned policy.

\section{Controller Development and Runtime Operation}

\subsection{CityLearn, EVLearn, and Experimental Development}
\label{sec:energaize-simulation}

Trial-and-error learning cannot begin on occupied buildings or connected vehicles. An exploratory charging action can violate a site limit, increase cost, or leave a user without the required departure energy. EnergAIze develops controllers against a simulated representation of the intended installations before considering real-world inference or actuation.

CityLearn provides the building, demand, photovoltaic, storage, weather, tariff, and community environment. EVLearn extends it with vehicles, chargers, connection sessions, battery dynamics, and V1G/V2G flexibility \parencite{nweye2025citylearnv2,fonseca2025evlearn}. Historical data processed through the integration path can populate this environment, while synthetic and hybrid scenarios expose controllers to reconfiguration, faults, tight limits, and rare events that are impractical or unsafe to create physically. Rule-based, optimisation, RL, and MARL controllers can then be compared under the same assets, information, time period, and KPI definitions.

The simulator is an experimental representation of selected physical mechanisms. It does not copy the complete physical system. Provider latency, rejected commands, unavailable vehicle telemetry, human overrides, and electrical protections require explicit disturbance models or separate real-world evidence. Chapter~\ref{chap:simulator} establishes the scenario and simulation contracts; Chapter~\ref{chap:real-world} identifies which assumptions survived interaction with external platforms and demonstrators.

Training and evaluation bind the controller configuration to a scenario, dataset, simulator version, seed, reward, and compute target. Logs, progress, KPIs, checkpoints, and the final policy artefacts remain associated with that job. This record allows a result to be reconstructed and prevents a model file from becoming detached from the world in which its behaviour was evaluated.

\subsection{Runtime Topologies and Deployment Boundary}
\label{sec:energaize-deployment}

An EnergAIze instance can execute centrally for several installations or locally beside one installation. Central execution suits a community platform that already aggregates participant data. Edge execution suits an installation requiring lower latency, reduced data movement, or continued local service during a cloud interruption. These placements implement the same logical control loop, but they have different connectivity, hardware, packaging, observation, and recovery requirements.

The model runtime and integration middleware remain separable. A policy bundle can be updated without replacing every provider adapter, while a new provider receiver does not require the controller to be retrained solely because the transport protocol changed. In OPEVA, OCPP links chargers and the charging platform; Modbus TCP/IP links selected chargers, meters, and local controllers; OCPI carries charging information and profiles between platforms; and REST or RabbitMQ connects data and algorithm services. The integration boundary absorbs these protocols and keeps them outside the policy.

The lifecycle infrastructure connects personal and institutional compute, remote HPC and GPU platforms, shared experiment storage, policy export, and one or more inference services. Its purpose is not merely to make training faster. It ensures that the configuration executed on heterogeneous workers produces a traceable artefact that can be validated and loaded by the runtime without a manual reimplementation of preprocessing or action semantics. Chapter~\ref{chap:lifecycle} develops this path and its failure semantics.

The presence of a component in the architecture does not assign the same evidence level to every site. An installation can contribute historical data, participate only in simulation, execute inference without actuation, or accept bounded physical commands. Chapter~\ref{chap:real-world} reports OPEVA and DemFlex by evidence level and keeps their deployment conditions separate.

\section{Architectural Rationale and Thesis Map}
\label{sec:energAIze-forward-map}
\label{sec:energaize-chapter-map}

The progressive views establish the architectural argument. Architectural View I showed that a useful local decision already depends on robust information treatment, explicit safety boundaries, and an executable runtime contract. Architectural View II showed that a community multiplies the number of decisions, interface diversity, coordination, shared constraints, computing demands, and deployment targets. Architectural View III assigned those pressures to explicit functional boundaries. EnergAIze provides the common system context in which the later contributions can be stated and evaluated. Evidence that the four requirements have been satisfied must still come from the experiments and demonstrators.

\section{Chapter Summary}

EnergAIze connects heterogeneous energy and mobility information to a supervised physical decision. Its architecture becomes necessary progressively. A single decision exposes robust information treatment, physical and user safety, and an executable deployment contract. A distributed community adds multidimensional scale, heterogeneous interfaces, shared constraints, reconfiguration, and heterogeneous computing targets. Percepta, prediction, PulseCharge, the Algorithms and inference services, the Fail-Safe Supervisor, CityLearn and EVLearn, and the experiment lifecycle give these responsibilities explicit boundaries.

The framework integrates the research context while the methods and evidence remain the responsibility of the later chapters. The next chapter begins that evidence chain by establishing the data, scenario, and simulation foundation used to train and compare controllers under the conditions revealed by the architectural views.
