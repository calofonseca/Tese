\section{Business-as-Usual References}
\label{sec:control-progression}

Energy management begins with an installation that already serves its occupants. Household demand follows daily routines, a connected vehicle begins to charge, and an appliance runs when its user requests it. Where \gls{pv} generation and a stationary battery are available, surplus solar energy can be stored during the day and used when consumption rises later. These decisions may be made independently by the equipment's existing settings, without a controller anticipating the needs of the whole building or \gls{rec}.

\gls{bau} represents this conventional operation in the simulator. Its connection to the real-world problem lies in the equipment, operating practices, and services that precede the proposed control. The Portugal collection supplies the corresponding consumption, generation, tariffs, and user requirements through the data preparation described in Chapter~\ref{chap:simulator}. The resulting reference is a simulation of a specified operating practice, against which the additional value of management can be assessed.

The vehicle introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} provides a useful example. It arrives in the evening and starts charging immediately, alongside the household's other demand. A stationary battery may already contain energy stored from the day's solar production and supply part of that demand. An improved controller inherits both the vehicle's departure obligation and the useful operation already provided by the battery. Its opportunity is to organise those resources more effectively over time.

\subsection{BAU with and without Storage}
\label{sec:control-bau-variants}

Two references separate the contribution of stationary storage from the contribution of more deliberate scheduling. \gls{bau} without storage leaves the stationary battery inactive. It represents the same consumption and service needs being met without that resource participating in the energy balance. \gls{bau} with storage enables ordinary \gls{pv} self-consumption: surplus production charges the battery, and sufficiently high local import triggers discharge within the configured power and state-of-charge limits. \gls{ev} charging and appliance operation retain the same rules in both variants.

Savings can then be related to anticipation, allocation of available power, or coordination, with ordinary battery use already included in the starting point.

The storage rule responds to the local energy balance. It does not consult the electricity price. An evening discharge may coincide with an expensive interval because household demand is high then; deliberately retaining energy for that interval requires the price-aware reasoning introduced in Section~\ref{sec:rbc-ladder}.

Immediate \gls{ev} charging also gives the reference a distinctive service outcome. The vehicle charges towards full charge even when its declared departure target is lower. It can therefore meet its minimum requirement while finishing outside the target band. The scorecard retains both minimum service and target accuracy so that a controller's savings can be read alongside the charge actually delivered to the user.

\section{Rule-Based Control and Optimisation}
\label{sec:rbc-ladder}

Ordinary operation establishes what an installation already does. A reference controller asks how much of the remaining opportunity can be captured by explicit decisions. Rules express practical knowledge about prices, deadlines, and available power. Optimisation considers those decisions together over a horizon. Both provide useful operating strategies, as well as demanding comparisons for learning.

The first EnergAIze study explored decentralised control with \gls{maddpg} and rule-guided exploration in an hourly, seventeen-member \gls{rec} \parencite{fonseca2024energAIze}. Subsequent development broadened the controller family and made service obligations and electrical constraints more explicit. The references described here preserve that history while giving the current studies a common basis.

\subsection{Rule-Based Control and Its Variants}

The original \gls{rbc} prioritised \gls{pv} availability and the vehicle's departure requirement. Charging became urgent when the energy still required approached the amount that could be delivered before departure; otherwise, the controller could wait for a more favourable opportunity. This supplied an interpretable controller and a source of useful early experience for the first learning experiments. The later family extends the same practical reasoning across stationary batteries, \gls{ev} charging, and deferrable appliances.

\gls{rbc} Basic introduces price and deadline awareness. It favours charging in cheaper periods and preserves service as a deadline approaches. Stationary storage responds to cheap and expensive conditions within its bounds. Its attraction is the modest information and tuning needed to make decisions more purposeful than immediate service. Its thresholds also make its behaviour easy to inspect: a change in charging time can be related to a price condition or an approaching obligation.

The Smart family adds local \gls{pv} production, demand, forecasts, and electrical headroom. Consider a vehicle needing 12~kWh during an eight-hour connection. A low current price alone does not determine whether it should charge immediately. The controller can also consider expected solar production, the power used by other equipment, and the time left after postponing the decision. When only two hours remain, the same energy deficit has a different operational meaning. The Smart rules follow this changing room for manoeuvre and coordinate service decisions with stationary storage.

\gls{rbc} Smart Local fixes a strict information boundary within that family. It uses the building's measurements and requirements together with permitted public signals, such as the tariff and weather forecast. Another building's private state and raw \gls{rec} aggregates are excluded. This distinction matters because the generic Smart implementation can use \gls{rec} information when a corresponding local signal is absent. Smart Local supplies the matched reference and teacher for experiments that claim building-local execution.

\gls{rbc} Community extends the decision context to \gls{rec} import, surplus, and operating pressure. A local battery may respond differently when several buildings are drawing power together or when renewable production is available elsewhere. The extension changes the information supporting the decision; it also creates dependencies on the availability and interpretation of the aggregate signals. In the current dataset setting, internal trading is disabled, so \gls{rec} awareness is assessed through scheduling and aggregate exchange rather than attributed to an unmodelled settlement benefit.

Signal-aware variants allow a higher-level coordinator to influence a rule through an effective price. A neutral signal recovers the unmodified condition; other values can encourage or discourage flexible consumption. These variants retain the underlying resource and service logic. Their role is developed with the hierarchy in Section~\ref{sec:hrl}, where the signal's authority can be assessed against the same local controller.

The variants require progressively more context and more decisions about priorities. Their cost remains largely the evaluation of explicit rules, although the supporting forecasts and \gls{rec} services have their own computational and communication demands. The most informed variant need not dominate every installation: inaccurate forecasts or unavailable aggregate data can remove its expected advantage. A meaningful comparison therefore identifies the information actually supplied and examines service, cost, and action corrections together.

\subsection{Optimisation over a Scheduling Horizon}
\label{sec:milp-control}

\gls{milp} places the resource decisions inside a common scheduling problem. At each interval, continuous variables represent grid import and export, battery charging and discharging, and stored energy. \gls{ev} variables exist over their connection sessions. Where required, binary variables prevent incompatible modes or select the start of a complete appliance programme. Once started, the programme contributes its specified power profile to the subsequent intervals.

\begin{samepage}
The formulation connects those decisions through a power balance. For one building and interval, a compact expression is
\begin{equation}
 p^{\mathrm{imp}}_{i,t}+p^{\mathrm{PV}}_{i,t}+p^{\mathrm{dis}}_{i,t}
 =p^{\mathrm{load}}_{i,t}+p^{\mathrm{EV}}_{i,t}+p^{\mathrm{app}}_{i,t}
 +p^{\mathrm{ch}}_{i,t}+p^{\mathrm{exp}}_{i,t}.
 \label{eq:control-milp-balance}
\end{equation}
Here, $i$ identifies the building and $t$ the decision interval. The terms $p^{\mathrm{imp}}$ and $p^{\mathrm{exp}}$ are grid import and export, $p^{\mathrm{PV}}$ is \gls{pv} production, $p^{\mathrm{load}}$ is inflexible demand, and $p^{\mathrm{EV}}$ and $p^{\mathrm{app}}$ are \gls{ev} charging and scheduled appliance demand. Stationary-battery charging and discharging are $p^{\mathrm{ch}}$ and $p^{\mathrm{dis}}$. All terms are non-negative powers in kW; the current comparison permits charging-only \gls{ev} operation.
\end{samepage}

\begin{samepage}
Battery decisions also couple consecutive intervals:
\begin{equation}
 E_{i,t+1}=E_{i,t}
 +\eta^{\mathrm{ch}}_i p^{\mathrm{ch}}_{i,t}\Delta t
 -\frac{p^{\mathrm{dis}}_{i,t}\Delta t}{\eta^{\mathrm{dis}}_i}.
 \label{eq:control-milp-storage}
\end{equation}
Here, $E_{i,t}$ is the stationary battery's stored energy in kWh, $\eta^{\mathrm{ch}}_i$ and $\eta^{\mathrm{dis}}_i$ are its dimensionless charging and discharging efficiencies, and $\Delta t$ is the interval duration in hours. The charging and discharging powers have the meanings defined above. Energy and power bounds restrict the schedule, while the initial energy and declared terminal condition determine how the horizon begins and ends.
\end{samepage}

Connection windows, available charging power, departure requirements, and appliance deadlines give the remaining variables their service meaning. Electrical constraints limit the combinations that can operate concurrently, including the represented phase limits. For the running example, the optimiser must find enough charging opportunities before departure while accounting for household demand and the stationary battery. A low-cost interval is useful only to the extent that its available power can deliver the required energy.

\begin{samepage}
The purchase objective is
\begin{equation}
 \min \sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}
 \lambda_t p^{\mathrm{imp}}_{i,t}\Delta t.
 \label{eq:control-milp-local-objective}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathcal{T}$ is the scheduling horizon, $\mathcal{N}$ is the set of buildings included in the problem, $\lambda_t$ is the grid-import tariff in EUR/kWh, $p^{\mathrm{imp}}_{i,t}$ is import power in kW, and $\Delta t$ is the interval duration in hours. The current setting assigns zero export revenue. The implemented service-first formulation first minimises unavoidable \gls{ev} departure shortfall and then minimises purchase cost subject to that service result.
\end{samepage}

Two choices determine the question answered by a solve. The first is its decision scope: local or \gls{rec}-wide, and stationary storage alone or the full set of flexible resources. Fixing the \gls{ev} and appliance trajectories isolates the remaining storage opportunity; optimising those services jointly gives a broader scheduling problem. The second is its information horizon. A perfect-information solve receives realised future demand, production, prices, and sessions. An operational rolling-horizon controller receives causal forecasts, applies an initial portion of its schedule, and solves again as new information arrives. It must also carry the energy state and unfinished services across successive solves.

\section{Local Reinforcement Learning}
\label{sec:control-local-learning}
\label{sec:single-agent-learning}

Once a strong rule-based reference is available, learning can be examined against decisions that already make physical sense. The household controller must discover when flexibility is valuable, how much charging can be postponed, and when an attractive action now has an undesirable consequence later. The first learning formulation keeps that question within one building. The agent encounters different prices, demand, and charging opportunities during development, then applies the learned policy to the reserved test year. This establishes what local learning can contribute before joint training is introduced.

\subsection{The Building as a Learning Agent}

One agent controls the flexible resources assigned to one building. A battery and two chargers therefore produce several decisions for the same agent; they do not automatically become three independent learners. This organisation lets the building policy account for the headroom shared by its resources and the service requirements that compete for it. Other buildings remain part of the simulated environment, with their own demand and decisions.

An observation describes what the agent can use when making that decision. Current demand and \gls{pv} production explain the immediate energy balance. Battery state and charging availability describe the resources that can respond. A vehicle's required charge and time to departure explain the service still to be delivered. Prices and causal forecasts supply context for comparing present and future use of flexibility. Table~\ref{tab:control-observation-roles} groups these inputs by their role in the decision.

The observation encoder turns these quantities into the policy's input. Cyclic time features preserve the proximity of the end and beginning of a day; categorical indicators distinguish connection and operating states; continuous features use the declared scaling. A missing measurement also needs an explicit interpretation. A missing battery state and a battery with zero available energy are different conditions, even if an incautious preprocessing step would map both to zero.

Figure~\ref{fig:control-local-agent} follows the local interaction. The observation is prepared at stage~1, passed to the actor at stage~2, and converted into resource commands at stage~3. The environment applies those commands subject to the resource and electrical models, then returns the next observation and reward. During learning, the resulting experience updates the policy and its value estimates. During evaluation, the selected policy acts with learning disabled.

Training uses the 2023 periods assigned to learning in Section~\ref{sec:sim-temporal-protocol}. Demand, production, and user requirements supply the circumstances of each interaction; the agent's own decisions determine the resulting battery states, delivered services, and rewards. The experience used for learning is therefore generated by interaction with the simulator. Reserved seasonal blocks support validation, while the complete 2024 sequence tests the selected policy with its parameters and preprocessing fixed. Training returns describe how learning develops; the annual test scorecard describes the operation obtained after that development.

\begin{samepage}
The fixed-layout actor can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\mathbf a}_{i,t}=\pi_{\theta_i}\!\left(f_i(o_{i,t})\right),
 \qquad
 \mathbf a^{\mathrm{app}}_{i,t}=\mathcal{E}_{i,t}
 \!\left(\widehat{\mathbf a}_{i,t}\right).
 \label{eq:control-local-action}
\end{equation}
Here, $i$ identifies the building, $t$ the decision interval, $o_{i,t}$ its permitted observation, and $f_i$ the observation encoder. The actor $\pi_{\theta_i}$ has learned parameters $\theta_i$ and produces the proposed action vector $\widehat{\mathbf a}_{i,t}$. The execution map $\mathcal{E}_{i,t}$ includes the configured local action handling and the environment's physical response, producing the applied vector $\mathbf a^{\mathrm{app}}_{i,t}$. For a stochastic actor, the first expression denotes an action drawn from its policy distribution; evaluation fixes the declared action-selection convention.
\end{samepage}

Each action entry has a resource-specific meaning. Stationary storage can charge or discharge, the current \gls{ev} setting permits charging, and an appliance command requests a programme start when allowed. A bounded neural output must therefore be mapped to the corresponding range and resource. The stored transition retains the action delivered at the learner--environment boundary, while applied-action records reveal subsequent adjustment. Keeping both makes it possible to distinguish a policy's proposal from a feasible outcome supplied by the simulator.

The local reward follows the building's objective, with service and constraint terms defined by the selected profile. One local \gls{ppo} or \gls{td3} learner is instantiated for each controlled building, with separate value learning and experience. Private observations from other buildings do not enter the actor. This provides a direct operational interpretation: the policy can make its local decision from the same information boundary used during development.

\subsection{PPO and TD3 as Local Alternatives}

Two methods examine different ways of using the interaction. \gls{ppo} learns a stochastic policy from recent trajectories and limits the size of its policy update \parencite{schulman2017ppo}. Its local value network estimates the future return associated with an observation. For the household example, the collected sequence links an evening charging decision to the eventual departure, allowing a later consequence to inform the earlier action.

\gls{td3} learns a deterministic actor from a replay buffer \parencite{fujimoto2018td3}. Two critics estimate action value; a conservative target, smoothed target actions, and less frequent actor updates moderate the influence of optimistic estimates. Replay allows the method to revisit a departure event while learning from later days. That reuse is attractive when unusual service events occupy only a small part of an annual trajectory.

The methods differ in their interaction and update patterns as well as their policy distributions. \gls{ppo} refreshes its learning data as the policy changes. \gls{td3} can make several updates from stored experience, whose relevance depends on the states, actions, and operating conditions represented. The comparison therefore records environment interactions, update counts, and computational demand separately. Equal interaction counts do not imply equal training effort.

Both local methods retain a fixed observation and action layout in this first study. Their actor networks map the declared input vector through hidden layers to the resource commands; their local critics supply training information and remain outside ordinary inference. The comparison holds the information boundary, service criteria, and evaluation conditions constant. It asks whether adaptation improves on the strong local rule and whether the improvement survives a complete service-aware reading.

\section{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning}
\label{sec:control-marl}
\label{sec:marl-progression}
\label{sec:standard-marl}

Consider two households that both postpone charging until the same low-price interval. Each decision may reduce a local bill, yet their combined demand can create a sharper \gls{rec} peak. Local adaptation alone gives each learner limited knowledge of the decisions that produced that peak. Multi-agent training makes those interactions part of the learning problem while retaining the building as the unit of operational control.

\subsection{Local Actors and Centralised Training}

\gls{ctde} separates the information used to improve a policy from the information needed to apply it. Each actor receives its permitted local observation and issues its building's resource commands. During training, the critics can additionally receive the declared joint observations and actions. Figure~\ref{fig:control-marl-training} shows this distinction: the training process can examine a simultaneous \gls{rec} decision, while each deployed actor retains a local inference path.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering

The joint context helps a critic distinguish outcomes that look similar from one building alone. A rise in \gls{rec} import may follow that building's charging action, the simultaneous actions of its neighbours, or both. Including those actions in value estimation makes their influence available during training. The training context is the information explicitly assembled for the experiment; concatenating observations need not reveal every hidden physical state.

Coordinated training also requires an objective that values the intended interaction. If rewards contain only uncoupled local purchases and the environment imposes no shared consequence, a central critic alone does not create an incentive to reduce the \gls{rec} peak. Local cost, \gls{rec} exchange, service, and electrical pressure therefore retain explicit roles in the reward configuration. Comparisons between learning rules hold those roles constant, while reward changes are examined separately in Section~\ref{sec:learning-efficiency}.

This arrangement fits the architecture in Chapter~\ref{chap:energAIze}. The local actor can be exported without the central critic, reducing its runtime dependencies. Observation encoding, action interpretation, retained rules, and local protection still accompany the evaluated controller. Decentralised execution consequently simplifies placement at the installation, while the training process can remain more demanding in memory, data access, and computation.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Actor--Critic Methods}
\label{sec:control-actor-critic-methods}

The learning rule determines how joint experience is used to improve each building's decisions. The methods considered here differ in the experience retained for training, the value estimates guiding their actors, and the way exploration enters the policy. These choices affect both the resulting control and the effort needed to obtain it.

\paragraph{MADDPG.}
The original EnergAIze formulation used \gls{maddpg} \parencite{lowe2017maddpg,fonseca2024energAIze}. Each building has a local actor, while its critic evaluates the joint decision during training. The critic can then relate a charging action to what the other buildings were doing. After training, each actor executes from its declared local inputs, fitting the runtime architecture described in Chapter~\ref{chap:energAIze}.

An optimistic critic may assign excessive value to an action combination, encouraging the actor to repeat it. Reward scale and exploration influence which combinations enter replay, and the joint input grows with the represented \gls{rec}. These sensitivities motivate examination of the critic estimates alongside the resulting control.

\paragraph{MATD3.}
\gls{matd3} retains decentralised deterministic actors while introducing twin critics, target smoothing, and delayed actor updates \parencite{ackermann2019matd3}. These changes address the influence of optimistic value estimates on policy learning.

\begin{samepage}
The \gls{matd3} training target uses the lower of the two target-critic estimates:
\begin{equation}
 y_{i,t}=r_{i,t}+\gamma(1-d_t)
 \min_{j\in\{1,2\}}Q^{\mathrm{target}}_{i,j}
 \bigl(s_{t+1},\widetilde{\mathbf a}_{t+1}\bigr).
 \label{eq:control-matd3-target}
\end{equation}
Here, $y_{i,t}$ is the target value for agent $i$ at interval $t$, $r_{i,t}$ is its reward, $\gamma$ is the discount factor, and $d_t$ identifies a terminal transition. The joint training state is $s_{t+1}$, $Q^{\mathrm{target}}_{i,j}$ is target critic $j$, and $\widetilde{\mathbf a}_{t+1}$ contains the target actors' actions with bounded smoothing noise. Taking the lower estimate reduces the influence of optimistic value estimates; delayed actor updates give the critics additional updates between changes to the policy.
\end{samepage}

\paragraph{MAPPO.}
\gls{mappo} uses local actors alongside a critic with central training information \parencite{yu2022mappo}. The shared training context can account for \gls{rec} interactions, while \gls{ppo}'s trajectory-based updates offer an alternative to repeated replay. \gls{ippo} retains local value estimation and supplies the corresponding comparison without that central information. The ensemble of independent \gls{ppo} learners in Section~\ref{sec:control-local-learning} fulfils this role in the strict local setting.

\paragraph{HAPPO.}
\gls{happo} examines sequential policy updates \parencite{kuba2022happo}. Updating one actor changes the decision context of the others, so the method accounts for that progression during optimisation. This is relevant when buildings have different equipment and contribute different forms of flexibility. It changes the update organisation while retaining the need for local actors, a declared training context, and an objective that reflects the \gls{rec} task.

\paragraph{Entropy-Regularised Control.}
The entropy-regularised alternative uses \gls{masac}, extending the actor--critic principle of \textcite{haarnoja2018sac} to the joint training setting. Its stochastic actors balance expected return with a preference for maintaining action diversity during learning. This provides a different exploration mechanism from adding noise to a deterministic actor. Entropy weights, reward scale, and action bounds consequently form part of its configuration. The underlying entropy-regularised learning principle is introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:foundations}.

\section{Controller Design and Training Strategies}
\label{sec:data-efficient-hierarchy}
\label{sec:learning-efficiency}
\label{sec:control-learning-interface}

The \glspl{rbc} already express useful knowledge about charging deadlines, electrical headroom, and ordinary storage behaviour. Asking a learner to rediscover all of that knowledge increases the amount of interaction needed before it produces plausible decisions. The next part of the investigation therefore considers how rules and recorded trajectories can support learning, and how the learning objective can preserve the services that make an economic improvement useful.

\subsection{Observation and Action Design}

Learning quality depends on the problem presented to the network. An observation containing a departure requirement but no time to departure omits the urgency that gives the requirement its immediate meaning. A large vector containing every available signal can instead make useful structure difficult to identify and increases the preprocessing and training burden. The developed observation profiles move from broad compatibility views towards compact operational inputs: remaining service, connection state, feasible power, headroom, and selected causal forecasts.

Action design raises a related question. A single output layer can produce all resource commands, while separate output heads allow different resource groups to develop more specialised mappings from a shared representation. Battery dispatch, continuous \gls{ev} charging, and an appliance-start request have different consequences even when each is represented by a bounded number. The comparison retains the same external command meanings while examining how the actor organises their prediction.

These choices are especially relevant to deployability. The feature order, scaling, missing-value convention, and mapping from output entries to equipment are needed whenever the model runs. A compact network reduces only part of the execution cost if observation preparation remains expensive or if the runtime cannot reconstruct the required information. The evaluation therefore follows the complete path from available data to requested actions.

\subsection{Guided Exploration, Demonstrations, and Residual Control}

Guided exploration uses a rule-based action during selected early interactions or blends it with the learned action while the policy develops. As the learner gains authority, it encounters a wider range of decisions. For the arriving vehicle, this can keep early experience close to a service-respecting trajectory, allowing learning to focus on when charging is valuable rather than spending most of its initial interactions on disconnected chargers or missed departures.

Demonstrations use the rule in another way. Observation--action pairs from a teacher train the actor to reproduce a useful starting behaviour. Subsequent interaction then allows the policy to adapt beyond the examples it has seen. Optimisation schedules can also supply demonstrations, with their information assumptions and development-period boundaries retained. An expert with future knowledge supplies training examples whose operational value still has to be assessed with causal observations at evaluation time.

\begin{samepage}
Residual control retains the rule during operation and learns a bounded correction:
\begin{equation}
 a_{i,t}=\Pi_{i,t}\!\left(b_i(o_{i,t})+g_i\odot\delta_{\theta_i}(o_{i,t})\right).
 \label{eq:control-residual-policy}
\end{equation}
Here, $a_{i,t}$ is the requested action of building $i$, $o_{i,t}$ is its observation, $b_i$ is the base controller, and $\delta_{\theta_i}$ is the learned correction with parameters $\theta_i$. The vector $g_i$ limits the authority of each correction, $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication, and $\Pi_{i,t}$ applies the configured local feasibility bounds at interval $t$. A zero correction recovers the base controller. The particular action dimensions assigned to the rule and the residual are part of the experiment.
\end{samepage}

This arrangement is useful when a transparent service policy already works well. The base can protect charging and appliance obligations while the learned component adjusts the use of flexibility. It also changes what must be deployed: the actor, base rule, action scaling, and local feasibility component collectively produce the evaluated behaviour. Packaging that composition preserves the relationship between development and runtime established in Chapter~\ref{chap:energAIze}.

\subsection{Reward Design and Experience Sampling}
\label{sec:control-reward-lineage}
\label{sec:control-replay-engineering}

A reward based too heavily on immediate energy cost can favour postponing charge until the departure requirement is missed. The electricity bill falls while the vehicle receives less service. Penalising every temporary charge deficit creates the opposite pressure: the learner charges as soon as the vehicle arrives, using little of the flexibility that motivated the control problem. The useful distinction is between a deficit that can still be recovered and an obligation that is becoming impossible to meet.

The controller development therefore examined the economic term alongside electrical pressure, service shortfall, and resource use. Departure consequences remain visible, while intermediate terms account for remaining connection time and deliverable charging power. If headroom contracts because another appliance is running, the amount of energy that can still be supplied before departure changes. The learner then faces a different decision even when the vehicle's current \gls{soc} is unchanged.

The scorecard keeps a stable meaning while these learning signals are investigated. A smoothed reward may provide a more useful training signal, but the final comparison still reports actual cost, minimum service, target accuracy, electrical violations, and grid shape. Battery throughput helps explain whether an improvement relies on more frequent cycling. This separation allows a reward change to be judged through the behaviour it produces.

Replay provides another way to make important consequences visible. Ordinary low-demand intervals can greatly outnumber departures, concurrent charging events, or restricted-headroom periods. The reward, exploration strategy, and replay distribution interact. A service penalty that is useful under uniform sampling may become dominant when service events are sampled more often. The \gls{rq} is whether a more useful distribution of experience improves the resulting controller and reduces the interaction needed to obtain it.

These mechanisms connect safety and scalability within learning itself. Service-aware experience can reduce damaging proposals, and useful demonstrations can reduce the amount of simulation needed. Their dependence on a particular observation and action description also brings deployability back into view. Experience from one charger arrangement can be reused only when the receiving policy can interpret the resources and commands it describes.

\section{Offline Reinforcement Learning}
\label{sec:control-offline}

A deployed controller accumulates a record of decisions and their consequences. The same is true of an \gls{rbc} running through a simulated year. These records create another route to learning: prepare a policy from existing experience before allowing it to interact with the environment. For an installation where exploratory actions can affect comfort, charging service, or equipment limits, that separation has practical value.

\subsection{Operational Data for Policy Learning}

An offline dataset contains transitions collected by a behaviour policy. For an energy installation, the record needs to associate an observation with the submitted action, its reward, and the next observation, while retaining the timing, resource configuration, and relevant execution information. A departure event then remains connected to the charging decisions that preceded it. The archive's usefulness depends on both the quality of those records and the range of conditions that produced them.

\gls{bc}, already used for pretraining above, learns to reproduce the recorded actions. Offline \gls{rl} uses recorded rewards and transitions to learn which behaviour is valuable \parencite{levine2020offline}. The distinction matters when the archive contains competent but imperfect operation: reproducing it supplies an initial policy, whereas value learning seeks opportunities to improve decisions within the support offered by the data.

A strong rule-based behaviour policy offers reliable services and repeatable trajectories, but it also shapes what the learner sees. If the rule almost always charges a vehicle immediately after arrival, the archive may contain little evidence about postponing that charge. The data can be extensive in time while narrow in action coverage. Combining compatible records from different policies, seasons, or operating conditions can broaden that coverage, provided their information and command meanings are retained.

This also explains the connection to the operational records in Chapter~\ref{chap:energAIze}. When the Fail-Safe Supervisor limits an action or provides a fallback, the archive should retain that intervention together with the policy's proposal and the action submitted for execution. The resulting outcome belongs to the complete control process. Using those records for later training requires choosing a consistent learner--environment boundary so that a critic does not attribute the fallback's result to an action that was never submitted.

\subsection{Offline Value Learning and Dataset Coverage}

Two established approaches clarify the main methodological choice. \gls{iql} estimates values from actions observed in the dataset and extracts a policy through advantage-weighted imitation \parencite{kostrikov2021iql}. Its value-learning procedure avoids querying unseen actions to construct its policy-improvement target. This offers a way to favour valuable behaviour while reducing one source of unsupported extrapolation.

\gls{cql} instead regularises action-value estimates to discourage optimism about actions poorly supported by the dataset \parencite{kumar2020cql}. Its conservative term changes how value learning treats the difference between observed behaviour and candidate actions. The practical question is how that caution affects useful scheduling: strong conservatism can restrict improvement, while insufficient caution can favour actions whose consequences are poorly known.

These approaches provide context for the offline investigation; their precise role in the collaborative study remains to be confirmed in the editorial point above. Their evaluation has two useful references. The behaviour policy identifies whether the learned controller improves on the operation that generated the archive. The matched \gls{bau} reference gives the improvement its physical and economic context. The data-collection cost is retained separately from the offline training budget.

Generalisation also needs a concrete interpretation. A new year changes weather, demand, and mobility within an existing installation. A second building with compatible inputs and commands tests reuse across installations. Adding an unfamiliar resource changes the decision description itself. Those situations impose different demands on the learned model and on the archive from which it was prepared. Holding out time periods alone assesses the first of them.

\section{Hierarchical Community Coordination}
\label{sec:hrl}

A capable local controller resolves many of the decisions inside a building. \gls{rec} coordination can then concentrate on how those decisions interact. This suggests a decomposition in which a \gls{rec} controller sends a compact signal and the building controllers retain responsibility for their resources and services. The investigation asks whether that smaller decision can improve a \gls{rec} outcome without requiring one policy to command every charger, battery, and appliance directly.

\subsection{Separating Community and Building Decisions}

The hierarchy contains a \gls{rec} coordinator and one local controller per building, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:hierarchical-control-architecture}. The coordinator receives aggregate conditions and issues a signal at a declared interval. A local controller continues to act at every fifteen-minute simulator step, using the signal alongside its permitted local information. An hourly coordinator decision, for example, is held for four local steps, leaving the building controller to respond to arrivals and changes in headroom during that hour.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{control-hierarchy.pdf}
\caption{A \gls{rec} signal influences local scheduling. Resource commands remain with the building controllers.}
\label{fig:hierarchical-control-architecture}
\end{figure}

The experimental signal is an effective price multiplier. A value of one preserves the unmodified local price condition. A lower value can encourage flexible consumption, while a higher value can encourage postponement or stationary-battery support when the local controller has the corresponding freedom. The participant's actual bill continues to use the original tariff; the multiplier is an input to the decision process. This distinction preserves the economic comparison when the coordinator changes its signal.

For the running example, the coordinator might encourage demand during a period of \gls{rec} \gls{pv} surplus. The household can respond only if a vehicle is present, a battery has available capacity, or an appliance can start within its permitted window. A household close to a departure deadline may have little freedom to reduce charging. The response therefore depends on local circumstances that remain the responsibility of the building controller.

A single multiplier for the whole \gls{rec} gives the coordinator a fixed-size output. Member-specific multipliers allow more selective use of flexibility, but the output then depends on the represented members. These two designs expose a direct trade-off between a compact \gls{rec} decision and more precise allocation. They also make distributional outcomes relevant: aggregate savings can be concentrated in the buildings with the most usable flexibility.

The causal path of the signal is checked before learning the coordinator. The neutral signal should reproduce the standalone local controller. Fixed non-neutral signals then establish whether the intended local decisions actually change. In the price-aware residual composition, the signal conditions the local base controller while the learned residual retains its original observation convention. This gives the coordinator a defined route to influence actions while preserving the meaning of the residual's inputs.

These comparisons use frozen local controllers so that the effect of the coordinator can be interpreted separately. Joint adaptation of both levels is another \gls{rq} because changes in the local policy alter the response being learned at \gls{rec} level. The fixed-local-policy study provides a clear starting point for understanding whether the signal has useful authority.

\section{Control under Resource Reconfiguration}
\label{sec:control-dynamic-resources}
\label{sec:control-transformer-ppo}
\label{sec:control-transformer-matd3}

The preceding methods improve decisions for a described installation. The installation itself can also change. A household may add a second charger, remove a battery, or replace equipment; a \gls{rec} may gain another member. The next investigation asks how a trained controller can continue to operate when the number of resources it observes and controls changes. That operational question motivates the representation choices that follow.

\subsection{Availability Changes and Structural Changes}

An \gls{ev} disconnecting from an existing charger changes availability. If the controller already has an input and output for that charger, it can represent the event through connection state and admissible action bounds. Installing an additional charger changes the number of independently addressable resources. A fixed output layer that previously produced one charger command now needs to produce two. The distinction separates a changing value in an existing description from a change to the description itself.

A fixed vector can reserve positions for a maximum number of resources and mask the unused entries. This is a useful solution when that maximum and the resource catalogue are known in advance. Its cost and coverage depend on the chosen capacity, and each reserved position still needs a stable interpretation. A variable-size policy instead processes the active resource records and produces a corresponding set of commands. Figure~\ref{fig:control-dynamic-resources} illustrates the question with a second charger of a known type.

The requirements extend beyond accepting a longer input. Each controllable resource needs its own output, and that output must return to the correct equipment. A decision also needs context: two chargers can have different departure obligations while sharing the same building headroom. The representation must therefore retain individual information and allow interactions among the resources relevant to a decision.

\subsection{Variable-Size Policies and Their Operational Scope}

Attention-based representations offer one way to meet those requirements \parencite{vaswani2017attention,lee2019settransformer}. Each resource contributes a feature record, which a type-specific projection maps to a common embedding size. Context records supply information such as building demand or price. The attention layers combine these representations, and output heads generate commands for the controllable resources. A second charger adds another compatible record and another associated command, while reusing the learned processing weights.

The \gls{ppo} variant applies this representation within each building's policy and value-learning process. The \gls{matd3} variant combines local resource-based actors with central twin critics. For critic learning, building-level summaries of resources and actions are aggregated to represent the joint decision. The learning rules retain their earlier purposes; the representation now addresses variability in the resources those rules process.

The current action catalogue covers stationary storage and \gls{ev} chargers; appliance information supplies context. A matched comparison keeps appliance service under the same declared rule when that resource lies outside the learned action catalogue. This preserves the decision scope while examining the representation.

The comparison with a fixed-layout actor asks two questions. First, how much control quality and computation change when the original installation is retained. Second, what preparation is needed when compatible resources are added or removed. A compact fixed-layout actor can be effective for its original installation. The additional structure is useful when it reduces the rebuilding, retraining, or remapping needed for another supported configuration.

The implementation also has to preserve the meaning of earlier experience. An off-policy transition describes the arrangement of resources and actions present when it was collected. Layout signatures group compatible replay records so that historical charger commands remain associated with their corresponding inputs. In the current Transformer controller implementations, changes among known resource instances can be represented within existing buildings, while a change in the building population rebuilds the learned controller state. Variable local resource counts and uninterrupted reuse across a changing population are therefore separate capabilities.

\section{State-of-the-Art Methods for Real-World Control}
\label{sec:control-sota}
\label{sec:control-variable-references}

Applying a learned controller to a \gls{rec} involves several responsibilities at once. It must coordinate the resources with acceptable computation, be practical to install and maintain, respect physical and service constraints, and retain useful behaviour when information or equipment becomes unreliable. These are the scalability, deployability, safety, and robustness problems defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction}. The methods reviewed in Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work} offer different responses to them, including responses developed outside energy systems.

The running household illustrates why their comparison belongs together. Reusing a policy for a second charger can reduce preparation and training, but both chargers must still receive the correct commands and share the available headroom. If one vehicle's measurement becomes stale, the controller must decide which services can continue. A method's practical value depends on this complete sequence, including the integration and supervision that support it. The following reference designs examine the mechanisms involved under the same \gls{rec}-control requirements.

\subsection{Task-Open Coordination and Compositional Learning}
\label{sec:control-sota-coordination}

MOHITO represents coordination through relations between agents, tasks, and actions. Its evolving hypergraph allows the actor and critic to account for tasks that enter or leave and for the corresponding available actions \parencite{anil2025mohito}. This supplies an algorithmic response to changing work, with the reported evidence coming from ridesharing and wildfire suppression. Its relevance to the thesis lies in coordinating decisions while service demands change, and in the reusable structure through which those decisions are represented.

For an energy adaptation, a building can be an agent and an \gls{ev} session a task carrying an energy requirement and departure time. The charger identifies the equipment through which a decision is executed. Constructing the hypergraph requires defining which agent can serve a task and what each available charging action means. A power discretisation must retain its physical resolution in the comparison, while a continuous-action extension must be identified as an adaptation. The arrival of a vehicle then updates service demand without being confused with the installation of another charger or the admission of another building.

This mapping connects the method to all four assessment dimensions. Computation and policy reuse concern scalability; the work of binding tasks and actions to equipment concerns deployability. Applied commands and departure service expose the consequences of the accompanying constraint handling. Fault experiments examine what remains operational when an agent, measurement, or actuator becomes unavailable. Each result belongs to the evaluated combination of policy and supporting mechanisms, with the published task-open scope retained explicitly.

Compositional learning separates another part of the problem. \gls{coin} uses specialised perception and action modules around a shared controller, learning combinations of observation, action, and instruction spaces through \gls{bc} \parencite{luketina2025compositional}. The modular construction provides a reference for reusing learned processing when the way a system is observed or actuated changes.

In the household, perception modules can prepare measurements from meters, batteries, and chargers, while action modules translate the common decision representation into the corresponding commands. A second installation can reuse familiar modules in another combination. Its integration still needs resource identities, units, ranges, and equipment associations. A \gls{coin}-inspired \gls{ppo} reference would test that modular principle under the thesis's learning protocol; it would be labelled as an adaptation because the published learner uses imitation.

The assessment follows both the reuse obtained and the operation delivered. Preparation and adaptation costs accompany energy performance; safety measurements follow the output through its protection and execution; disturbance tests retain service and recovery records. Familiar modules offer a useful basis for deployment, while their compatibility with the installation remains an explicit condition of the experiment.

\subsection{Constraint Handling and Fault-Tolerant Learning}
\label{sec:control-sota-protection}

An algorithmic comparator can also act on the decisions produced by an otherwise unchanged policy. Continuous-action safety layers solve a state-dependent correction problem before a proposal is applied \parencite{dalal2018safe}. For the two chargers, the comparison follows how a requested power is modified when simultaneous charging exceeds the available headroom. The protection may preserve the electrical limit while postponing service, so correction magnitude and missed departures accompany the violation count.

Multi-agent shielding addresses the additional coupling between local decisions. Certified combinations of local obligations can support a shared safety specification when their joint assumptions hold \parencite{elsayedaly2021shielding,adalat2026contracts}. This provides a reference for distinguishing a building's own limit from a transformer limit shared across the \gls{rec}. In a matched energy study, the physical model, allocation assumptions, admissible actions, and fallback authority must be declared. They determine both the guarantee and the coordination freedom left to the learner.

Fault-tolerant learning addresses the behaviour learned before a failure occurs. \gls{marta} augments a \gls{marl} learner with selective fault induction: a switching mechanism chooses the affected agent and timing, while an adversary supplies faulty behaviour \parencite{mguni2025faulttolerant}. The comparison with ordinary disturbance training concerns where adverse experience is concentrated and how the resulting policy behaves under faults. In the \gls{rec} setting, an adaptation must state whether a fault affects an agent's decision, its observation, or the equipment response; these events have different physical consequences.

A robustness comparison therefore holds the fault exposure and information available to the controllers explicit. It retains service deficits, failed decisions, supervisory intervention, and recovery, together with nominal performance. Training cost also matters: improved fault tolerance may require additional interaction or adversarial optimisation. The same experiment thus connects robustness and safety to the computation required for learning and the mechanisms needed to maintain operation.

\subsection{The Remaining Research Problem}

The four research dimensions established in Chapters~\ref{chap:introduction} and~\ref{chap:related-work} are connected by the same operational setting. A controller must remain computationally manageable, be practical to place in an installation, respect its resources and services, and retain useful behaviour under disturbance. The methods investigated here offer useful capabilities at each of those points. Sustaining them as the installation changes requires the policy, resource description, feasibility handling, and execution path to continue referring to the same control problem.

Return to the household with a stationary battery and two chargers. Adding a third charger of a familiar type requires an observation record, a command, and a place within the shared headroom. Reusing a trained resource encoder addresses part of that change. The action mapping and feasibility calculation also need to recognise the new charger, while replay and model packaging need to retain a compatible interpretation. If its state measurement later fails, the affected decisions need an appropriate response without unnecessarily stopping the rest of the building.

The unresolved question is how learned coordination and its supporting mechanisms can jointly maintain performance with manageable computation and integration effort, admissible actions, and useful service under disturbance.
